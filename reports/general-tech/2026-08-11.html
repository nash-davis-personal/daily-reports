◆ The security item to act on

Breaking Aug 10

Metabase's password-reset endpoint was an unauthenticated SQL injection, and someone found it first

The flaw sits in the publicly reachable POST /api/session/reset_password endpoint and carries a CVSS of 10.0. It affects every release from 1.58 onward, spanning the 0.58 through 0.63 branches, and it is tracked as GHSA-vwf4-m7j8-wcjf. Exploiting it gives a remote, unauthenticated attacker full administrator access to an instance, which in turn exposes the stored credentials for every database that instance connects to, plus config changes and bulk export.

Metabase found out the hard way. Its own Metabase Cloud platform was breached on August 3 by someone using the then-unknown flaw against customer instances. Metabase blocked the endpoints, shipped a patch within hours and auto-upgraded all cloud accounts. Fixed releases are x.58.24, x.59.21, x.60.17, x.61.11, x.62.9 and x.63.5. Self-hosted instances are the ones still exposed.

So what for TSD: Patch instruction: if you or any client runs self-hosted Metabase, upgrade to the fixed release for your branch today, then rotate every database credential that instance held — the patch does not undo stolen credentials. Broader lesson for client builds: an internal BI tool reachable from the internet is a full-database credential store with a login form in front of it. Put those behind Vercel access control or a VPN, not just a password.
Metabase advisory · The Hacker News
Breach Aug 10

Framework, Tally and Kilo Code all lost customer data through the same vendor

Framework Computer said Metabase notified it on August 6 that its instance had been vulnerable and had been accessed on August 3. Exposed fields: names, email addresses, physical addresses, phone numbers and login IP addresses. Tally, the Belgian form-builder, disclosed email addresses and password hashes. Kilo Code, the AI coding platform now owned by Anaconda, disclosed names, emails and customers' Slack access tokens, which it invalidated immediately.

None of the three was itself breached. Each was breached through a dashboard tool it had pointed at its own production database.

So what for TSD: This is a sales argument, not just a warning. Every SMB client you have runs at least one third-party tool with a live connection into their data, and none of them have an inventory of which ones. A one-page "what has read access to your database" audit is a real, chargeable first engagement and it opens the door to a retainer.
Help Net Security · BleepingComputer

◆ Silicon keeps getting funded at a scale that is hard to picture

New Aug 10

Intel sold $20 billion of stock, its first public share sale since 1971

Intel announced a $15 billion underwritten offering Monday morning and priced it that evening at $20 billion: 210,526,315 shares at $95, roughly $19.7 billion net, with the deal expected to close August 12. Underwriters hold a 30-day option for more. Proceeds go to general corporate purposes, meaning the capital expenditure behind the foundry buildout.

The stock fell about 4% on the announcement, which is the ordinary reaction to roughly 3% dilution. It is worth holding both facts at once: Intel is diluting shareholders because the stock has surged around 175% this year, and it is doing so to fund capacity customers are asking for.

So what for TSD: No action. It is a read on where the AI buildout is in its cycle — a company that avoided public equity raises for 55 years just decided the demand signal was worth the dilution. Compute supply keeps expanding, which is good for your input costs eventually and bad for anyone whose moat is access to GPUs.

◆ Your stack

Clear Aug 11

No new Next.js, React, Vercel, Tailwind or Supabase advisory in this window

Nothing new landed on the stack you ship on. The standing items are unchanged: the React and Next.js unauthenticated RCE pair in Server Function endpoint deserialization (CVE-2025-55182 and CVE-2025-66478), the memory-exhaustion DoS in React and Next.js (CVE-2026-23864), and the SSRF in WebSocket upgrade handling that only affects self-hosted Next.js on the built-in Node server. Vercel-hosted projects get platform-level blocking on the RCE request patterns, which is not a reason to skip the upgrade.

So what for TSD: Quiet day means it is a cheap day to run npm audit across the client portfolio and clear the backlog. The SSRF one is the only item where self-hosting versus Vercel actually changes your exposure, and everything you run is on Vercel.
