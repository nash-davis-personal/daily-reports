The memory shortage has a lobbying budget now

The RAM crunch created by AI data centers has escalated from a procurement headache into a Washington fight, with Apple pressing for clearance to buy from a blacklisted Chinese memory maker and finding both unsympathetic officials and a technical dead end: customizing those chips means handing over details that US tech-transfer rules forbid. Around it, capital kept committing to silicon — $6.3 billion from Sony and TSMC for a Kumamoto sensor fab, $3.5 billion from South Korea for its supply chain — while the Royal Navy quietly disconnected its drone cameras from the internet.

◆ Memory is the constraint, and it is now political

Policy August 10

Apple wants blacklisted Chinese RAM, and both Washington and physics are in the way

AI data centers have absorbed so much memory manufacturing capacity that consumer and enterprise buyers are competing for what is left, and the resulting price spiral has pushed the issue into Washington. The New York Times reports a rush of lobbying around the shortage, with many US officials showing little sympathy for Apple's position. Apple has spent months pressing for clearance to buy from ChangXin Memory Technologies, a Chinese maker on a US blacklist, including appeals from Tim Cook to administration officials. A bipartisan group of senators has already written to Apple urging it to drop the effort.

The Wall Street Journal adds the detail that makes this harder than a permission slip. Apple is testing CXMT's RAM, but Apple does not use commodity parts — it wants customized versions, and specifying those means transferring technical detail to CXMT. US tech-transfer rules prohibit that. So even a favorable political outcome leaves Apple with generic chips rather than the parts it designs around. Meanwhile the shortage keeps feeding through to prices: PC and phone makers have been explicit that memory costs are behind recent increases.

So what for TSD: The direct effect on you is hardware budgets. If a Mac, a dev machine or a client's on-prem box is on the list for this year, the price curve is against waiting, and the same is true for any client you would put a local model on — that machine needs a lot of RAM and RAM is the scarce thing. It is also a useful counter-argument in a sales conversation: "run it on your own hardware" is getting more expensive to say yes to, which quietly strengthens the hosted-service version of every offer you sell.

Capex August 10

Sony and TSMC commit $6.3B to a Kumamoto sensor fab, and Seoul funds the layer nobody photographs

Sony and TSMC will spend around ¥1 trillion, about $6.32 billion, on a joint venture to mass-produce next-generation image sensor chips in Kumamoto prefecture in southern Japan. Ownership runs roughly 60% Sony and 40% TSMC, with commercial production starting as early as 2029. The two announced the intent to form the venture in May; this is the number attached. Sony is the largest image sensor maker in the world and TSMC the largest contract manufacturer, and both have pointed at physical AI — automotive and robotics — as the demand they are building for.

South Korea moved on a different part of the chain the same day, with Reuters reporting a roughly $3.5 billion fund for materials, parts, equipment and fabless companies, plus acceleration of domestic chip hubs. Fabs get the headlines; the chemicals, tools and design houses feeding them are where a supply chain actually breaks.

So what for TSD: No immediate action, but it dates the horizon. Sensor capacity aimed at robotics and automotive arriving in 2029 tells you the physical-AI wave is a late-decade commercial story, so do not let a client talk you into building for it now. The near-term read is the same as the memory story: silicon stays tight and expensive for years, which is an argument for pricing AI work as a service with usage terms.

◆ Hardware you did not audit is hardware you do not control

Security August 9

Royal Navy drones were phoning an IP address in China, so the Navy cut the line

The Telegraph reports that UK Royal Navy surveillance drones contained Chinese-made camera components that were sending what the reporting describes as "heartbeat communications" to an IP address in China. The response was to remove internet connectivity from the drones' cameras. The components were inside a platform the Navy had already bought and deployed, which is the part worth noting: the problem was not a compromised network but an ordinary supply chain that nobody had traced to the module level.

So what for TSD: Scale it down and it is your problem too. Every npm package, every third-party script tag and every embedded widget on a client site makes outbound calls you have not personally read. A Content Security Policy on client sites that names the domains allowed to receive requests is the cheap equivalent of pulling the cable, and it takes an afternoon to add across the portfolio.

◆ Platforms rewriting the deal

Product test August 9

Spotify is testing a button that skips the ads publishers sold themselves

Semafor reports Spotify is testing a skip control that lets Premium subscribers jump past publisher-sold advertising inside podcasts. Audio networks have raised concerns, because host-read and publisher-sold spots are the revenue that funds most independent shows, and a skip button converts a guaranteed impression into an optional one.

So what for TSD: Relevant to your own podcast engine. Build for a world where the ad slot is skippable, which means the value of a show sits in the content and the call to action woven into it rather than in a detachable read. For client work, the general lesson applies to any business whose distribution runs through someone else's app: the platform can change the economics without asking, which is the whole argument for owned channels — email lists, their own site, their own booking flow.
Semafor via Techmeme
Funding August 10

Palmer Luckey's Erebor is raising $1.5B at an $8B valuation to be the new SVB

The Financial Times reports Erebor, the lender Palmer Luckey started to fill the hole left by Silicon Valley Bank, is in advanced talks to raise roughly $1.5 billion at an $8 billion valuation. Startup banking has been an unsettled market since SVB collapsed, and an $8 billion private valuation on a lender is a bet that the gap is still unclosed.

So what for TSD: Worth tracking only in the sense that your payment rail has been a bottleneck. More banks courting small technical companies means more competition for the account and the merchant processing, so it is reasonable to revisit that decision this quarter rather than accept the first thing that approved you.
Financial Times via Techmeme
