Build one on-prem AI box for the practices that will not touch the cloud

The shift: Google converted Gemma-4-26B-A4B into DiffusionGemma for under ten percent of the original training token budget. It runs at roughly 1,500 tokens per second on an H100, the weights are Apache 2.0 on Hugging Face, and its speed advantage is specifically a single-user phenomenon that disappears around 32 concurrent requests. source ↗ today's AI brief

Why you: The concurrency ceiling that kills this for a shared Concierge endpoint is exactly what makes it right for a single professional at a desk. Charlotte is full of small law firms, accounting practices and medical offices that have said no to AI on one objection: the data leaves the building. Apache 2.0 weights on hardware they own answers that objection completely, and there is no per-token bill to erode the margin later. Nobody else calling on those offices can build it.
First move: Do not buy hardware. Stand up DiffusionGemma on a rented GPU hour this week and record a four-minute screen capture of it drafting from a document with the network visibly off. That video is the entire sale. Take it to one accounting practice before tax-season planning starts and quote the box plus a setup fee plus a support retainer, with the client buying the hardware directly.

Run a reverse-proxy review for the legacy clients you inherited

The shift: James Kettle at PortSwigger fed 138 HTTP and SMTP RFCs into HTTP Terminator, generated 30,000 candidate attack vectors, and found roughly 700 vulnerable targets across 30,000 authorized test sites including banks, government infrastructure and an airport. One technique alone exposed over 200 sites. The tool is open source. source ↗

Why you: Your Vercel-hosted sites terminate at an edge you do not control, so this is not a fire drill for your own portfolio. It is a precise, narrow sale to the clients who came to you with an existing self-hosted stack and an Nginx or Apache proxy sitting in front of an old app. You can scope it honestly because you know exactly which clients those are, and "an open-source tool now finds this at internet scale" is a fact rather than a scare.
First move: List every client running their own reverse proxy. It is probably three or four names. Offer each a fixed-fee review that applies PortSwigger's own guidance: avoid HTTP/1.1 upstream, and where that is not possible, allow-list methods at both layers and restrict which methods may carry a request body. One afternoon each, one invoice each.
