◆ The capital story got its headline number

Markets Aug 25, 2026

Anthropic is expected to show investors a $30 trillion addressable market

The Wall Street Journal reported that Anthropic will tell IPO investors its potential revenue opportunity exceeds $30 trillion, topping the $28.5 trillion SpaceX used ahead of its own offering. The estimate is built on the full scope of work that could be done by AI models rather than on any current spending category. Against it: the company more than doubled revenue to $11.6 billion in the second quarter.

The rest of the reported terms are a raise of up to $100 billion at a valuation near $2 trillion, with prospectus documents expected shortly and a debut possible as early as September or early October. NYU's Aswath Damodaran, who called the SpaceX version of this number "reaching the end of what's plausible and pushing beyond," is on the record as a skeptic. SpaceX priced at $135 and traded below $105 intraday in August before recovering, which is the cautionary comparison sitting right next to the pitch.

So what for TSD: A TAM defined as "all work that could be done by a model" is the same argument you make to an SMB owner when you sell TSD Front Desk — you are not competing for their website budget, you are competing for the cost of the labor it replaces. Steal the framing. The number itself is unfalsifiable and repeating it makes you sound like a pitch deck. The practical watch item is the timing: if a prospectus lands in September, Anthropic's actual revenue mix by product line becomes public, which is the first real data on what people pay for agent and API work versus chat seats. That is a pricing input for your Managed AI retainers.

Earnings Aug 26, 2026

MiniMax posted $116.6 million for the half, with enterprise up more than 700%

The Shanghai lab reported first-half 2026 revenue of $116.6 million, a 283% year-over-year increase. The composition matters more than the total: Open Platform and AI-based enterprise services went from $9.2 million to $73.9 million, more than 703% growth, and now make up 63.4% of revenue against 30.3% a year earlier. The half represents roughly 32% of the $363.77 million analysts expect for the full year, so the run rate is behind consensus even at that growth rate.

MiniMax shipped M3 during the period, aimed at coding and agentic workflows. Vercel is serving M3 and M2.7 free on its AI Gateway through September 6 via GMI Cloud, under the minimax/minimax-m3-free and minimax/minimax-m2.7-free model IDs, which is a cheap way to see whether the coding claims hold up on your own work.

So what for TSD: A Chinese lab making two-thirds of its money from API and enterprise rather than consumer chat is the same shape as your business, at 1000x scale. Worth thirty minutes: run one of your Concierge RAG prompts against minimax/minimax-m3-free on the Vercel gateway before September 6 and compare output quality and latency to what you pay Anthropic for the same call. If the gap is small on the easy half of your traffic, that is margin on every retainer.

◆ Chinese models are shopping for Western distribution

Distribution Aug 26, 2026

Moonshot is negotiating with Microsoft, Amazon and Google to host Kimi K3

Reuters reported that IPO-bound Moonshot AI is in preliminary talks with all three US hyperscalers over revenue-sharing arrangements that would put Kimi K3 on Azure, AWS and Google Cloud. Moonshot is seeking up to a 30% share of the revenue those platforms generate from K3-related services. Open questions include how revenue is split, what data access the clouds get, and how token usage gets audited.

Any signed deal would be the first large revenue-sharing pact between a Chinese AI company and a major American cloud. Moonshot has already done similar deals with smaller cloud platforms. The context is that Chinese models are materially cheaper than Western ones and are picking up US usage anyway, while Washington restricts the chip trade in the other direction.

So what for TSD: If K3 lands on Bedrock or Vertex, the cheap-model option stops being a procurement conversation and becomes a dropdown. That is good for your cost base and bad for your differentiation, because any competitor can then quote a lower price on the same infrastructure. Your defensible layer is not the model — it is the client's data, the integrations, and the fact that they own the source. Keep leading with that. Also expect at least one client to ask whether a Chinese model touches their data; have a one-paragraph answer ready before you get asked in a sales call.

Compute Aug 26, 2026

Huawei answered an Egyptian tender with 2,000-plus Ascend chips

Bloomberg reported that Huawei pitched the Egyptian government on building AI data centers for military, surveillance and other public-sector use. The proposal covers exporting 1,408 top-end Ascend 950-series chips for an AI training cloud, plus 600 more of either those or the earlier 910B for two inference clusters, on a stated 12-month build. The US government is reported to be working on a competing offer.

So what for TSD: No direct action. It matters because sovereign AI buildouts are the demand floor holding up the whole compute market that sets your API prices — the same buildout Nvidia reports against tonight. If that demand is shifting to non-US silicon in non-US markets, the long-run pricing picture for the models you resell gets more competitive, not less.

◆ The agent layer got identity and a number

Data Aug 26, 2026

Daily agent use among engineers nearly doubled in twelve months

Temporal's 2026 State of Development report, run by Qualtrics between April 29 and May 25 with 554 qualified engineer respondents, puts daily-or-more AI agent use at 80.8%, against 47.3% a year earlier. The more useful split is between engineers who have shipped an agent to production and those who have not: the former are 1.5x more likely to use agents daily (85.5% vs 55.8%) and 2.3x more likely to use them continuously (23.9% vs 10.4%). Successful teams also reach for agents in technical design, security and support, not only in code.

So what for TSD: This is a sales asset. When an SMB owner asks whether AI automation is real yet, "four out of five engineers now use an agent every day, up from half last year" is a cited, dated, third-party number that does not come from a vendor selling them something. Put it in the Grant and Bishop leave-behind. The second finding is a note to yourself: the gap is between people who shipped one and people who dabbled, which is exactly the gap your Managed AI retainer sells across.

Security Aug 24–26, 2026

Okta's Agent SSO puts AI agents in the same directory as employees

Okta made Agent SSO generally available, registering AI agents that use the Cross App Access protocol as identities in Universal Directory alongside human staff. Admins assign access policies to an agent the way they would to a person, and the agent gets short-lived tokens issued when it needs to act in another application on a user's behalf. Okta's own example is governing a deployed Claude instance. It is included at no additional cost on core SSO plans.

The problem it targets is the one every agent build hits: long-lived API keys pasted into configs, plus consent fatigue when a user is asked to approve the same connection repeatedly. Vercel shipped its own answer to the same problem this week with Connect, which issues scoped runtime credentials for agents rather than storing static tokens.

So what for TSD: Two vendors solving the same problem in the same week means static-credential agent architecture is now the thing an enterprise security review will flag. You are not selling into Okta shops today, but audit your own builds: anywhere Front Desk or Booking Bridge holds a long-lived third-party token, that is the pattern the industry is actively moving off. See today's Technology briefing for what Vercel Connect costs and covers.
