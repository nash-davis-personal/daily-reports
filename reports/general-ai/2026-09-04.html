◆ The frontier moved twice in two days

New Sept 3

OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra at $10 and $50 per million tokens

Astra went live Thursday with a 1M-token context window, multimodal input, and an optional fast mode that buys 2.5x speed at double the price, so $20 and $100 per million. Access started with organizations in OpenAI's Daybreak program; Plus, Pro, Business and Enterprise subscribers, the API and AWS were promised "in the coming days." The lineup is Astra and Astra Pro.

The benchmark story is narrower than the launch language. Astra's separation shows up in math and computer use: 97.6% on FrontierMath Tier 4 against 73.2% for Claude Opus 5 and 87.8% for Fable 5.1, and 41.4% on AutomationBench against 26.9% and 31.4%. On coding it is inside the noise, 74.1% on DeepSWE v1.1 against roughly 74% for both Opus 5 and Gemini 3.8 Flash. On Humanity's Last Exam with tools it loses, 57.2% against 63.6% for Opus 5 and 65.0% for Fable 5.1. Two caveats worth carrying: OpenAI funded FrontierMath and holds exclusive access to it, and the published scores were run at maximum effort, which is not how anyone runs a production app. There is also still no same-harness cross-vendor coding benchmark that includes Astra, so head-to-head coding tables are guesses dressed as data.

So what for TSD: Nothing in your delivery stack needs to change today. Astra is roughly 4x Opus 5-class pricing for a coding edge that does not exist yet in any comparable harness, and it is not even in the API. Where it is worth a test when the API opens is the computer-use and document-generation work — the AutomationBench gap is the one real jump — which maps to the booking-automation and back-office pieces of your SMB offers rather than to writing code.

New Sept 2–3

Meta's Muse Spark 1.3 puts a frontier coding score at $1.25 a million

Meta shipped Muse Spark 1.3 on September 2 and it reached the aggregators and Vercel's AI Gateway on September 3. Standard xhigh pricing is $1.25 per million input and $4.25 per million output, with a 1M-token context window and text, image and video input. There is a much cheaper contributor endpoint at roughly $0.10 and $0.20 that lets Meta train on your data, which is a hard no for client work.

On the numbers it is not a budget model. It scores 75.4% on DeepSWE v1.1 against 74.0% for Opus 5, ties GPT-5.6 Sol at 88.8% on Terminal-Bench 2.1, and leads long-context retrieval at 98.5% on MRCR 256K–512K and 98.1% on 512K–1M. Opus 5 still beats it on four of six agent evaluations, and the max reasoning tier is gated behind safety testing. The real catch is token accounting: 1.3 emits roughly 3x the output of 1.2, so the per-token price cut is smaller than it looks on the rate card. Weights are not open — Zuckerberg said an open-weight release is coming "soon" in a September 2 post with no date attached.

So what for TSD: This is the model to benchmark against Fable 5.1 for the high-volume, low-margin jobs inside your products — Concierge retrieval answers, transcript summarization, lead qualification — where you pay per call and the client pays a flat retainer. Run the same 50 real client prompts through both, count output tokens rather than trusting the rate card, and decide on cost per completed task. It is already callable through the Vercel AI Gateway key you have.

◆ The assistant era ends on Android

Breaking change Sept 4

Google starts shutting down Google Assistant today, with no way back

Google begins retiring Google Assistant on September 4 across Android phones and tablets, Wear OS watches, headphones and Android Auto, replacing it with Gemini. Google notified users by email. The rollout takes a few weeks to reach everyone, and once it lands on a device Assistant stops working with no option to revert. Assistant survives for now in cars with "Google built-in," while Gemini expands onto Google TV, Google Home speakers and smart displays. The shutdown was originally set for 2025 and slipped a year.

The open question is reliability rather than capability. Assistant handled routine commands — timers, smart-home toggles, calls — deterministically. Gemini handles them probabilistically. That trade is about to be tested on a few hundred million devices at once.

So what for TSD: Two concrete things. First, any client site with voice-triggered actions or an Assistant-based smart-display integration needs a check this month. Second, this is the clearest possible sales line for TSD Front Desk: the biggest assistant on earth just got swapped out from under its users without asking, and a business that owns its own phone agent does not get migrated by someone else's roadmap.

◆ Rules, and the products that enforce them

Policy Sept 4

About 30 California AI bills are on the Governor's desk until September 30

California's legislature closed its 2026 session on August 31 having passed roughly 30 AI-related measures. Governor Newsom has until September 30 to sign or veto. The ones with operational teeth for anyone deploying customer-facing AI are SB 1119 on chatbot safety, AB 1405 creating a registry of AI auditors, SB 813 on third-party verification organizations, and SB 947 on worker protections.

Other states are moving on their own clocks. New York's session closed June 1 with S 9051 on kids' chatbot safety and A 6578 on training-data transparency awaiting signature by December 31. New Jersey enacted its FAIR Act on algorithmic rent pricing July 20. North Carolina's H 301 cleared the Senate on June 10 and sits in conference committee. Pennsylvania's House reconvenes September 9.

So what for TSD: Chatbot-disclosure rules are the ones that touch you directly, because Front Desk and Concierge both talk to end customers. Get ahead of it cheaply: add a standing "you are speaking with an AI assistant" disclosure and a human-handoff path to every deployment now, and write it into the Managed AI retainer as a compliance line item rather than a freebie. NC's H 301 is worth a five-minute read since it is your home state and it is one vote from done.

Trend Sept 3–4

Gating what an agent is allowed to do became its own product category

Three launches in two days point the same direction. JetStream announced Clearance on September 3, a reasoning engine that evaluates and blocks dangerous agent actions before execution rather than logging them afterward. On September 4, Tenable launched CyberAgents Exchange AI Inspector to review third-party agent skills and playbooks before deployment, and Proofpoint shipped a SOC analyst agent that turns natural-language questions into structured investigation findings while keeping humans on the critical actions. Genesys added an AI Control Plane to its contact-center stack on September 3 for policy-aware sequencing and handoffs.

The common assumption across all four: agents are already in production, and the missing piece is not capability but permission.

So what for TSD: Package this rather than buy it. Every one of your AI deployments should ship with a written action allowlist — what the agent may book, refund, send or escalate, and what always routes to a human — plus a log the client can read. That document costs you an afternoon and it is the single easiest thing to charge for in a retainer, because it is the objection every SMB owner raises before they say yes.
