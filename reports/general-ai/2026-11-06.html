◆ The pace of progress

Research Nov 5

METR's independent assessment: Anthropic's capability progress has roughly doubled, and AI is most of the reason

From August 12 to October 29, METR staff worked inside Anthropic with internal pretraining and RL metrics, compute allocations, internal benchmark scores, speed-up analyses, forecasts and staff interviews. Their conclusion: as of October 2026, Anthropic is likely improving its models on well-specified tasks at around twice the rate it did between March 2024 and October 2025. The concrete number is a 1.9x acceleration in the AECI benchmark trend since October 2025 (95% CI 1.5–2.8x), which METR describes as roughly two years of historical progress in one year. They are confident the jump did not come from faster compute scaling, and a majority of it is likely attributable to Anthropic's heavy use of AI in R&D, mostly as assistance rather than fully autonomous work. They found no bottleneck in algorithms, training compute, experiment compute or data that would stop further acceleration in the next 12 months, and say the risk of even faster acceleration is significant.

The catch is transparency. METR says Anthropic redacted a majority of the evidence METR found most informative, citing confidential information that could accelerate other developers. METR publishes a table of what it could and could not show and states plainly that the public has to rely on METR's judgment more than is ideal. METR also disagrees with Anthropic's August Risk Report, which put acceleration below 2x; METR argues crossing the early-warning threshold was plausible. Anthropic says its own report is coming in the next few weeks.

So what for TSD: The models you build on are improving on a steeper curve than a year ago, and the lab says there is no visible brake. Plan products around capability you will have in six months, keep model calls behind a thin abstraction so swapping to the next Claude is a config change, and stop quoting clients fixed scopes that assume today's limits.

Model Nov 5

OpenAI released GPT-6 Cyber, rated Critical for cyber, to a vetted list of Daybreak Red customers

GPT-6 Cyber is a version of GPT-6 Astra built for authorized vulnerability research. It is the model that powered Wednesday's Linux kernel run, and OpenAI says security teams at Mozilla, Palo Alto Networks and Cisco have used it; results cited include 81 new high-severity Firefox vulnerabilities and 48.5% on ExploitGym. OpenAI classifies its cyber capability as Critical under its Preparedness Framework, a step up from High for GPT-5.6 Cyber, which is why access starts with a select group of approved Daybreak Red customers through the API and Codex, with identity verification and legal attestations. OpenAI says it will expand to all Daybreak Red customers soon, is opening GPT-5.6 Cyber to the whole tier, and created a Daybreak Red for Critical Infrastructure group for approved defenders.

So what for TSD: Exploit discovery at this level is now a product, which means the gap between a patch and a working exploit keeps shrinking for everyone, including the small-business sites you host. Automate dependency updates on every client Next.js and Supabase project, and make "we patch within 48 hours of an advisory" a written line in the Managed AI retainer.

◆ Anthropic's week: bank accounts in Claude, and three platform changes

Product Nov 5

Claude Personal Finance beta: link accounts through Plaid, ask about spending and bills, read-only

Anthropic started rolling out Personal Finance in beta this week to US subscribers on Pro, Max and Ultra. Users connect bank, credit card, loan and investment accounts through Plaid, which covers roughly 12,000 institutions, and ask Claude about balances, spending, upcoming bills or unusual transactions across up to two years of history. Access is read-only; Claude cannot move, manage or invest money. Anthropic says the hub carries no ads or sponsored recommendations, conversations inside it are excluded from model training, and it tells users to check important figures against their bank since balances can lag. It calls this a first step and says it is exploring partnerships with licensed professionals and regulated firms. Reuters framed the timing against the IPO; investor Anthony Pompliano accused Anthropic of copying his startup, which Anthropic has not answered publicly.

So what for TSD: Plaid plus a model is now a consumer feature from a frontier lab, so the "AI bookkeeper for one small business" product is a commodity idea by spring. The sellable version for your clients is the operator view: a Supabase-backed dashboard that reads a shop's Square and bank feeds and answers "what did Tuesday actually make after refunds," which Claude's consumer hub will never do per-business.

Platform Nov 2–5

On the Claude Developer Platform: message threads (beta), an IP allowlist (beta), and watermarked text

Three release-note entries this week matter to anyone running agents on the Messages API. Message threads (Nov 2, beta header message-threads-2026-08-12): the API keeps the conversation server-side, so each later request sends only the new messages plus the previous response id, which means smaller requests and lower time to first token. A thread expires 5 minutes after its last request and is deleted within an hour, so you still keep your own copy and need a replay path. Available on the Claude API, AWS, Google Cloud (Anthropic managed) and Microsoft Foundry. IP allowlist (Nov 5, beta): up to 1,000 IPv4/IPv6 addresses or CIDR ranges per organization; requests from anywhere else are refused, which caps the damage from a leaked key. Text watermark (Nov 3): output from Mythos 5, Opus 4.7 and Sonnet 4.6 now carries Anthropic's watermark on the platform, with a caveat for Sonnet 4.6 at non-default sampling settings. Also noted: fast mode for Opus 4.8 and the Sonnet 4.5 model both go away Nov 30.

So what for TSD: Two concrete to-dos. Put message threads behind a flag in TSD Front Desk and TSD Concierge where a call or chat runs many turns; the latency win is real but the 5-minute expiry means the replay path must exist before you turn it on. And add Vercel's egress IPs to an allowlist on each client org's key once you confirm the egress range is stable; it is the cheapest key-leak insurance you can sell.

◆ Cheaper video, and agents that ship whole apps

Model Nov 5

Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash is GA: 15-second clips, conversational edits, 30% cheaper at $0.07 a second

Google made Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash generally available on the Gemini API, Google Cloud, Google Vids and Gemini for business. Clips go to 15 seconds natively (up from 10 on Omni 1.1), with duration settable in one-second steps from 3 to 15. Multi-turn editing lets you change a background, lighting or aspect ratio with a follow-up prompt instead of regenerating. Reference adherence improved; Google's demo keeps one model's identity and three outfits consistent through a 15-second virtual try-on with 360-degree turns. Price drops 30% to $0.07 per second of 720p, about $1.05 for a full 15-second clip, with the suggested workflow of drafting at 360p or 720p and upscaling only the keeper to 1080p or 4K. Vercel put it on AI Gateway the same day, so one API key and spend tracking cover it.

So what for TSD: A :15 social spot draft now costs about a dollar in generation before upscaling, and the edit-by-prompt loop fits the way you already iterate on commercials. Run a side-by-side against the Seedance 2.5 takes on the next Higgsfield job for the cheap drafts and B-roll, and show Nash the frames; keep the hero shots on the pipeline that already works.

Agents Nov 5

Cursor "Build Live Apps": a cloud agent scaffolds the app, provisions Postgres and auth, and deploys to a link

Cursor's framing is that agents made code cheap but most tools never leave localhost. Now a cloud agent, told explicitly that you want an app, scaffolds a web app, spins up a Postgres database if needed, sets up authentication behind Cursor SSO, provisions hosting and returns a live link on a domain you pick. Deterministic and agentic security checks run before publish (secrets in files, exposed credentials), nothing goes live until you click publish, and team admins can see every app and secret, add review rules and unpublish. Each app runs in an isolated hosting account per team or user. Private apps sit behind Cursor sign-in; individual plans can publish public ones. Paid plans with cloud agents only. Separately, the clock is still running on OpenAI's proposed Nov 12 cutoff of its models to Cursor after the SpaceX acquisition; the final date was never confirmed publicly.

So what for TSD: Internal tools for a 20-person company just became a prompt inside a $20 editor, so stop selling "we'll build you a simple internal app" as a line item. Sell what Cursor cannot: the client's own data model in their own Supabase, custom auth for their staff and customers, and the integration work. And warn clients on Cursor that any OpenAI-model workflow may break next week.

◆ The buildout meets the public

Policy Nov 5

OpenAI's National Community Compact, Google's $17B Wyoming campus, and a DOJ Super Intelligence Task Force, all in one day

Two days after local voters backed limits in 40 of 41 data-center ballot measures, OpenAI published a National Community Compact: no nondisclosure agreements with public officials in data-center deals except narrow security and trade-secret carve-outs, full payment of electricity and grid-upgrade costs at sites it owns and operates, reduced peak-hour draw, power returned to the grid, and local hiring targets where feasible. The pledges are not legally binding and carry no dollar figures; OpenAI says it had planned them before the vote. It also released a Compute Campus Policy Blueprint asking states to require the same of every developer. The same day Google announced a $17 billion first data center in Laramie County, Wyoming, powered by a 702 MW natural-gas fuel-cell project (AEP contract, Bloom Energy cells) with emissions captured and stored at Tallgrass's sequestration hub, air cooling to cut water use, and Google covering the full cost of its electricity. And the Justice Department launched a Super Intelligence Task Force, led by Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward and Associate Deputy AG Stephen Weiss, focused on foreign-state misuse, transnational crime and AI-enabled fraud, linked to the White House's Super Intelligence Force; no investigative powers or cases were described. Markets reacted to the broader picture with a chip rout, covered in today's Technology and America briefs.

So what for TSD: The energy cost of AI is becoming a line the public reads, which lands as a sales objection sooner or later: "doesn't this stuff burn power?" Have the answer ready in a sentence (a receptionist call costs a fraction of a cent of compute) and keep it factual. No action beyond that; none of this changes your stack.
