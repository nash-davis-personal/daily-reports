◆ The economy and markets

Markets Nov 5

Wall Street's worst day since April 2025: S&P −4.36%, Nasdaq −5.52% into a correction, Dow −1,833

Thursday erased Wednesday's post-election rally and then some. The S&P 500 closed at 7,353.73, down 4.36%; the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.52% and is now more than 10% below its high, the definition of a correction; the Dow lost 1,833 points, or 3.44%. All three were the largest one-day drops since April 2025. The selling concentrated in semiconductors: the iShares Semiconductor ETF fell 12.77%, its worst session since March 2020, with Nvidia down about 9%, AMD about 17%, and Qualcomm sharply lower after its handset-chip revenue fell 26% on a 14-year low in global smartphone shipments. The VIX closed at 28.18. Two explanations ran through the coverage. Investors read Tuesday's results, including local ballot measures limiting data-center construction and candidates critical of the buildout taking office in states holding about 40% of planned US data-center capacity, as a sign the AI infrastructure build could slow or get more expensive. And Qualcomm's and Arm's outlooks landed on a market that had run hard. Nomura's strategist called it a hedge-fund momentum unwind rather than a policy call; JPMorgan said the AI boom remains on track but costlier and slower. Futures were mixed before Friday's open, with Nasdaq futures bouncing, and the rout spread to Asian chipmakers overnight.

Why it matters: For anyone managing their own money, a 4% index day after a 3% up day is what a concentrated market looks like: the top names set both the rally and the drop. The chip index is now well below where it started the week, while model prices and cloud earnings kept moving the other way the same day. Diversification and time horizon, not the headline, decide whether this was a problem.

Rates & energy Nov 5

Hike odds fell, the 2-year dropped 12 basis points, and crude rose nearly 5% against the tape

Money leaving stocks went into Treasuries. The 2-year yield fell 12 basis points to 4.46% and the 10-year finished at 4.84% on the official par row, down from 4.90% Wednesday and 4.95% Monday. Prediction-market and futures pricing for a December rate hike fell together: Kalshi's any-hike contract to about 64% from about 83% a day earlier, and the CME FedWatch probability to 70.1% from 82.7%, with no Fed speaker or data release named as the cause. The 10-year breakeven inflation rate slipped a third session to 2.24%. Crude did the opposite of equities: Brent rose 4.8% to $99.18 and WTI 3.5% to $91.21, reversing most of Wednesday's 8% drop, with coverage pointing to renewed uncertainty about US-Iran talks. Thursday's ISM services index came in at 54.5 with the prices component at 73.0. The Fed's weekly balance sheet showed the Treasury's cash balance at $936 billion and bank reserves at $2.96 trillion.

Why it matters: Mortgage and small-business loan rates follow the 10-year, which eased this week even as the hike odds for December stayed above even money. Gasoline follows Brent, which is a dollar from $100 again. The two are pulling household budgets in opposite directions.

Jobs Nov 6, 8:30 a.m.

October payrolls rose 165,000, nearly triple the forecast, and the unemployment rate fell to 4.1%

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported at 8:30 a.m. that nonfarm payrolls rose by 165,000 in October and the unemployment rate fell to 4.1%. The Dow Jones consensus had been 59,000 jobs with unemployment holding at 4.2%; FactSet was at 65,000 and Vanguard, the high outlier, near 120,000. September's initial reading was 29,000. The week's other labor readings had leaned the same way: initial jobless claims fell to 189,000 for the week ended Oct 31 against 197,000 expected, Challenger's count of October announced layoffs was 43,994, down 71% from a year earlier, and ADP's private-payroll estimate was +86,000 with small businesses adding 28,000. Sector detail, wage growth and revisions to prior months were not yet in the first wire reports when this was filed; before the release the 10-year yield sat at 4.82–4.83%, its lowest since early September, Nasdaq-100 futures were up 1.6% and Dow futures down about 444 points.

Why it matters: A payroll number this far above consensus, with unemployment falling, is the kind of print that revives December hike expectations after Thursday's drift lower, and it argues that the labor market is holding up better than the September reading suggested. Watch the revisions to August and September when the full tables are read; a beat with large downward revisions reads differently from a clean beat.

Earnings Nov 5

Airbnb grew revenue 19% and authorized a $12 billion buyback; Cloudflare grew 39%; Qualcomm's phone chips fell 26%

Thursday's after-hours reports split along the same line as the trading day. Airbnb posted $4.87 billion in revenue, up 19%, its fastest growth in more than three years, raised full-year guidance a third time, reported $1.4 billion of free cash flow and a $12 billion repurchase authorization, and said an AI travel agent arrives in the first half of 2027. Cloudflare reported $782.5 million in revenue, up 39%, which its CEO called the strongest quarter in company history. Qualcomm's fiscal fourth quarter showed handset revenue of $5.14 billion, down 26%, as global smartphone shipments fell 20% in the quarter to a 14-year low and memory costs rose; at least eight brokerages cut their price targets. Five9 agreed to buy contact-center AI firm Level AI for about $325 million in cash.

Why it matters: Consumer travel and enterprise software are growing at double digits while the phone market shrinks; the selling on Thursday was about the hardware supply chain and its politics, not about demand across the economy.

◆ Policy with a concrete effect

Infrastructure Nov 5

After the data-center vote: OpenAI pledges no NDAs and full power costs, Google commits $17 billion to Wyoming, and the DOJ forms an AI task force

Local voters decided 41 data-center ballot measures on Tuesday across roughly ten states, Ohio alone accounting for about half; by one tally the anti-expansion side prevailed in 40 of them. Two days later OpenAI published a National Community Compact: it will stop using nondisclosure agreements with public officials in data-center negotiations (narrow exceptions for security and trade secrets), cover the full electricity and grid-upgrade costs at sites it owns and operates, reduce peak-hour draw, return power to the grid, and set local hiring targets where feasible. The pledges are not legally binding and list no dollar amounts; OpenAI says they were planned before the vote. It also issued a Compute Campus Policy Blueprint urging states to require the same of all developers. The same day Google announced its first Wyoming data center, a $17 billion project in Laramie County near Cheyenne, powered by a 702-megawatt natural-gas fuel-cell installation under a 20-year supply agreement with American Electric Power (about $4 billion of AEP investment) with emissions captured and stored at Tallgrass Energy's Eastern Wyoming hub and air cooling to limit water use; Google says it will pay the full cost of its electricity. Separately, the Justice Department announced a Super Intelligence Task Force led by Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward and Associate Deputy Attorney General Stephen Weiss, focused on misuse of advanced AI by foreign governments, criminal organizations and fraud, coordinating with the White House's Super Intelligence Force; no cases, powers or timeline were described.

Why it matters: Whether a data center raises a neighbor's power bill is now a question companies answer in writing before they build. For a small business, the practical effect is on the utility side: large-load customers paying their own grid upgrades is the arrangement regulators in several states have been moving toward, and the biggest buyers just volunteered for it.

◆ Weather and disasters

Flooding Nov 3–6

Florida's Space Coast and Treasure Coast: 28 inches of rain in Palm Bay, a seven-county emergency, schools closed through Friday

A slow-moving storm dropped more than 28 inches of rain on Palm Bay in Brevard County, which Fox Weather characterized as a 1,000-year rainfall event for the area, washing out roads, flooding homes and cars and knocking out water service in places. Governor Ron DeSantis declared a state of emergency for seven counties. Public schools closed in Brevard, DeSoto, Indian River and St. Lucie counties midweek; the Indian River and St. Lucie districts stayed closed Friday for a third day and plan to reopen Monday, Nov 9. St. Lucie County shut a stretch of South 25th Street between Fort Pierce Central High and Forest Grove Middle because the road is unsafe. Drier air was moving into South Florida by Thursday with rain chances near 20% on the east-central coast, but standing water and swollen drainage systems keep the flood threat in place for days.

Why it matters: Flood insurance claims and FEMA assistance in a non-hurricane event are the practical story; most homeowner policies exclude flooding, and the National Flood Insurance Program covers only those who bought it. Anyone with property or family on the Treasure Coast should document damage now.
