◆ The economy & the Fed

Today June 17, 2pm ET

Fed concludes its June meeting — first decision under Chair Kevin Warsh

The Federal Open Market Committee met June 16–17 and announces its decision at 2:00pm ET today. As of June 13, CME FedWatch put the odds of no change near 97%, which would keep the target range at 3.50–3.75% (effective rate ~3.62%). This is Kevin Warsh's first meeting as Fed chair, so the communication carries unusual weight: the updated "dot plot" of rate projections and his first press conference will signal whether the committee's bias is tilting from easing toward neutral or tightening. The backdrop is awkward — May CPI rose to 4.2% annually (highest since April 2023) on higher energy costs, even as Q1 GDP grew a solid 2.0%.

Why it matters: The rate sets the cost of borrowing for everyone — credit cards, auto loans, small-business lines, and mortgages. A hold keeps financing roughly where it is; the projections and tone hint at where rates head next. For anyone managing their own money or running a small business, the dot plot and Warsh's first remarks are the real signal, not the (likely unchanged) number.

Markets May–June 2026

Energy keeps inflation hot; crude stays above $100

Higher oil and gas prices — Brent crude has held above $100 per barrel — pushed the Consumer Price Index to a 4.2% annual rate in May. The energy spike traces to conflict in the Middle East earlier this year; the US–Iran ceasefire is currently holding, which has kept prices from climbing further. Some economists now think the Fed's next move could be a hike rather than a cut if inflation persists.

Why it matters: Gas and energy costs feed directly into household budgets and small-business expenses (delivery, utilities, materials). Persistent 4%+ inflation is the main thing that could keep rates higher for longer — the opposite of the rate-cut path many borrowers were counting on.

◆ National & public safety

Incident June 15, 2026

B-52 bomber crashes at Edwards Air Force Base; 8 killed

A Boeing B-52H Stratofortress crashed shortly after takeoff near Edwards Air Force Base, California, on June 15, killing all eight aboard. The aircraft (assigned to the 412th Test Wing) was on a test flight for a radar-modernization program; the crew included military personnel, government civilians, and contractors, two of them Boeing employees. The cause is not yet known, and officials estimate the investigation could take about six months. It is the first B-52 crash since 2016 and the deadliest since 1982.

Why it matters: A rare and serious loss of life in the military test community, and a setback for the B-52 modernization effort. Edwards is a major flight-test hub; a six-month investigation may slow related test programs.

◆ Charlotte / North Carolina

Local June 2026

Charlotte lands a Japanese bank's second US HQ and a wealth-management hub

SMBC Group chose Charlotte for its second US headquarters, bringing about 2,000 jobs and a $50.5 million investment. Separately, AssetMark Financial will invest $10 million to establish an East Coast hub in Charlotte's central business district, creating 252 new jobs. The wins fit a broader pattern: North Carolina was named CNBC's top state for business for the third time in four years, and its unemployment rate remains below the national average.

Why it matters: More financial-services headcount in uptown Charlotte means more well-paid professionals and the businesses that serve them — directly relevant to local SMBs and anyone selling services in the metro. A growing, lower-unemployment regional economy is a tailwind for new local demand.
