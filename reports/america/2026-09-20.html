◆ The Fed turned

Breaking September 16

FOMC lifts the target range to 3.75–4.00 percent on a unanimous vote

The Committee raised the target range for the federal funds rate by one quarter percentage point to 3-3/4 to 4 percent, voting 12-0. The statement said inflation remains elevated and that the action "will support a timelier return to the Committee's 2 percent goal." On employment it recorded that job gains have kept pace with the workforce and the unemployment rate has changed little.

It is the first increase in more than three years. Chair Kevin Warsh used the press conference to emphasize persistent inflation, with core PCE running near 3.4 percent against headline measures around 3.7 percent. Updated projections show a strong majority of participants — 16 of 18 — viewing another increase as possible before year end, and futures markets moved to price a further quarter point in December with additional tightening extending into 2027.

Why it matters: Borrowing costs for households and businesses are now set to rise rather than fall. Variable-rate business credit lines, equipment financing and commercial mortgages reprice upward from here, and anyone who had been waiting for cheaper money before making a purchase is now waiting on a curve that points the other way.

Markets September 16–17

Stocks dropped on the decision, yields rose, and the dollar firmed

The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 631.21 points, or 1.21 percent, on decision day. The S&P 500 closed down 0.45 percent at 7,551.81 and the Nasdaq Composite finished essentially flat at 25,978.42, down 0.01 percent. The two-year Treasury yield rose more than seven basis points to 4.736 percent and the ten-year closed at 5.01 percent, with the twenty-year down one basis point to 5.39 percent.

Equities recovered the following session as oil prices and bond yields retreated. The wider read is that a renewed US tightening cycle points to a stronger dollar, more pressure on other currencies, and less room for central banks elsewhere to cut.

The macro backdrop: TSD's internal board reads the regime as "sovereign-supply, absorption held by the plumbing" — direction sideways, conviction medium — with the nearest alert-grade wires on the front end and the long end at once: the ten-year above 4.75 percent, the three-month above 3.75 percent, and the 2s10s curve at or under +40 basis points, which sat at +33. Its September hike-odds wire stood at 82 percent going into the meeting, and the hike landed. The next dated catalyst on the board is the core PPI twelve-month release on October 15 at 08:30 ET. Why it matters: A ten-year above 5 percent resets the discount rate under mortgages, corporate debt and equity valuations at the same time. For anyone managing their own money, the practical change is that cash and short-duration Treasuries now pay meaningfully more while long-duration assets carry more rate risk than they did a week ago.

◆ Small business and federal policy

Policy September 18

SBA waives most upfront fees on small-manufacturer loans

The Small Business Administration announced it will waive most upfront fees for small manufacturers in fiscal year 2026, charging no upfront fee on 7(a) manufacturing loans up to $950,000. It follows two earlier expansions this year: the cumulative 7(a) and 504 loan limit was doubled to $10 million in May, and small manufacturers became eligible to apply for $5 million through the 7(a) program in July.

Two dates sit alongside it. The SBA's August 20 proposed rule rewriting the size standards that determine which companies qualify as small for federal contracts, SBA-backed loans and other programs closes for public comment on September 21; current standards remain in effect until a final rule issues. And a new Standard Operating Procedure applies to applications issued an SBA loan number on or after October 1, 2026, with anything in process through September 30 continuing under current rules.

Why it matters: Manufacturing borrowers get a direct cost reduction on financing in the same month the Fed made money more expensive, which partly offsets the hike for that one category of business. For any company near a size-standard threshold, the comment window closing Monday and the October 1 SOP change are both worth a call to a lender this week.

◆ Jobs, prices and one very large listing

Economy Context for the hike

The labor market the Fed hiked into

Nonfarm payrolls rose 162,000 in August against a consensus near 53,000, with the unemployment rate unchanged at 4.1 percent. Bars and restaurants led job creation while information-sector employment fell. June was revised up 11,000 to +31,000 and July was revised up 44,000 to +21,000, putting the two months 55,000 higher than first reported. Labor-force participation edged up to 61.6 percent and average hourly earnings rose 0.3 percent to $37.75, up 3.1 percent year over year.

On prices, August CPI came in at 0.4 percent monthly and 3.4 percent annual for headline, with core at 0.3 percent monthly and 2.4 percent annual. Wage growth at 3.1 percent against headline inflation at 3.4 percent means real average hourly earnings went backwards over the year.

Why it matters: Employment held up well enough that the Committee could raise rates without arguing it was risking the labor market, which is the condition that makes a December increase plausible. For households, the gap between 3.1 percent wage growth and 3.4 percent headline inflation is the reason the economy can look solid in aggregate and feel tighter in a specific budget.

Corporate September 18–19

Anthropic moved its IPO to November with revenue reported above a $100 billion pace

Anthropic's annualized revenue is reported to exceed $100 billion this year, up from a $9 billion run rate at the end of 2025 and $65 billion at the end of July. The company moved its planned listing from October to November so third-quarter results can be presented to investors before shares trade, with reports putting investor interest around a $2 trillion valuation.

Growth is attributed to business customers using its software for coding and other workplace tasks. Nothing has been priced and no filing terms have been confirmed publicly.

Why it matters: A listing at that scale would be among the largest in US market history and would land in the same quarter the Fed is tightening, making it a live test of how much risk appetite remains for AI-linked assets. Index funds and retirement accounts would carry exposure to it whether or not an individual investor chooses to.
