◆ Markets and energy

Markets August 4

Both benchmarks set records as cheaper oil and strong earnings pulled the market up

The Dow Jones Industrial Average added more than 900 points, or 1.71%, to close at 54,086, its first close above 54,000. The S&P 500 rose 1.79% to 7,736.52, a record. The Nasdaq Composite gained 2.59%. Two things drove it: a batch of solid corporate earnings, with Palantir up 29% after reporting and Caterpillar also contributing, and the prospect of more crude reaching the market.

Futures pointed modestly higher Wednesday morning, with Dow futures up about 172 points, before the session lost momentum as investors worked through the latest technology results and oil turned back up.

Why it matters: Index records driven partly by an expected supply change in one waterway are contingent on that change actually happening. For anyone managing their own money, the useful read is that a large share of this move is an energy-price bet rather than a broad earnings revision, and it can reverse on the same news it rose on.
CNN Business TheStreet CNBC
Energy August 4–5

Crude hit a three-week low on talks over reopening the Strait of Hormuz, then rose again

Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent both said talks with Iran and Oman had progressed toward allowing more shipping through the strait, potentially within the week. Brent fell almost 5% to under $80 a barrel and West Texas Intermediate fell more than 5% to about $76, a three-week low. Rubio said no final agreement had been reached.

By Wednesday oil had turned higher again after a fresh threat against shipping in the region, and an attack on a vessel near the strait underscored how unsettled the situation is. Roughly a fifth of the world's oil and natural gas supply moves through the waterway in a normal year, which is why a partial reopening moves the global benchmark by several percent in a single session.

Why it matters: Fuel is a direct input cost for every trade, delivery and service business, and diesel and gasoline follow crude with a lag of a couple of weeks. A sustained move of this size shows up in freight surcharges and service-call pricing before it shows up in headline inflation.
The Irish Times The National The Washington Times
Earnings August 4

SpaceX beat expectations in its first quarter as a public company, and spent $18.4 billion doing it

Revenue for the June quarter came in at $7.8 billion against a $6.9 billion consensus, up from $4.1 billion a year earlier — a 92% increase, driven by Starlink and the company's AI businesses. The reported loss was 9 cents a share where analysts had modeled 26 cents, with a bottom-line loss of $541 million. Capital expenditure was $18.4 billion in the quarter, funding Starship, the next-generation Starlink V3 satellites and AI compute buildout.

The company told investors it expects a $100 billion annualized revenue run rate by the end of 2026. The stock had drifted well off its post-listing highs going into the report, and the size of the spending line is what the market is now weighing against the growth.

Why it matters: The first set of audited numbers from a company that was private for its entire history gives everyone else a real yardstick for satellite internet and launch economics. The capex figure also lands in the same debate as every other AI infrastructure builder: revenue is growing quickly and the spending required to sustain it is growing faster.
NPR The Washington Post US News

◆ Jobs

Labor Week of August 3

The labor data stack up Thursday and Friday against a slowing baseline

Weekly jobless claims arrive Thursday, with consensus near 202,000 initial claims and about 1.79 million continuing. The July employment report follows Friday. The last full ADP monthly print, for June, showed private employers adding 98,000 jobs, below May's 122,000 and under a roughly 110,000 forecast, with annual pay up 4.4% for job stayers and 6.6% for switchers. ADP's weekly preliminary series showed private employers averaging about 15,000 additions a week over the four weeks ending July 11, a fifth consecutive week of slowing.

The Federal Reserve is holding at 3.50–3.75%. Friday's number is the one that matters for the next meeting.

Why it matters: Hiring that slows while indexes set records is the tension in the current economy. For anyone selling to businesses, softer hiring usually shows up first as longer approval cycles on discretionary spending rather than as outright cancellations.

North Carolina August 4

1,622 people around Charlotte have lost jobs to noticed layoffs this year, up about 10%

WARN notices filed with the North Carolina Department of Commerce put Charlotte-region job cuts at 1,622 through July, roughly 10% above the same point in 2025. The two largest are outside the urban core: Family Dollar's distribution center in Matthews is closing with about 373 positions eliminated in phases, with the last separations around August 12, and Nippon Electric Glass is shutting its Shelby plant on August 31, ending nearly 70 years of operation there and displacing 282 workers.

The regional picture is split. Charlotte remains one of the faster-growing metros in the country on the strength of banking, healthcare and technology, while distribution and manufacturing employment in the surrounding counties contracts.

Why it matters: The losses are concentrated in logistics and manufacturing in Mecklenburg's neighboring counties rather than in Charlotte proper, which means the local effect is uneven — a Gastonia or Shelby small business feels this in its customer base well before the metro-level growth numbers show anything.
Kiss 95.1 (Charlotte) Spectrum News NC TheStreet

◆ Fire season

Disaster August 2–5

The Old Trails Fire has destroyed more than 640 structures in northwest Spokane

Three major fires are burning around Spokane. The Old Trails Fire alone has destroyed at least 640 structures with tens of thousands of residents under Level 3 "Go Now" evacuation orders, more than 5,000 structures still threatened, and power out to more than 13,000 homes. Governor Bob Ferguson declared a state of emergency and activated the National Guard, citing record drought and high winds.

Nationally the season is among the worst on record: 102 uncontained large fires are burning, including 33 in Oregon, 16 in Washington and 8 in Idaho, across more than 3.2 million acres.

Why it matters: Losses at this scale run through property insurance nationally, and a season this size pressures rates and coverage terms in fire-exposed states first and everywhere else eventually. The immediate effects are regional — air quality, power and road closures across the interior Northwest.
