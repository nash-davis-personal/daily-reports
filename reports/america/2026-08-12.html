◆ The economy

Data Aug 12, 8:30am ET

July CPI is the day's number, and gasoline cuts both ways inside it

The Bureau of Labor Statistics releases July consumer price data this morning. Forecasters expect headline CPI up 0.2% for the month, which would take the annual rate to 3.4% from 3.5%, and core CPI up 0.2% for the month with the annual rate easing to 2.5% from 2.6%. June's headline reading was an unexpected 0.4% decline, so a positive July print is a bounce rather than a break in trend.

Energy is doing most of the work. Gas prices fell early in July as crude dropped on optimism the Iran conflict was ending, then climbed again late in the month when fighting resumed, netting out to a projected 2% to 3% decline in the gasoline component. Projections also have inflation running ahead of wage growth for the month. The print feeds directly into expectations for the Fed's next rate decision, and into Treasury yields.

Why it matters: A 3.4% headline keeps inflation roughly a point and a half above the Fed's target, which argues against near-term rate relief on anything with a variable rate — business lines of credit, card balances, HELOCs. For anyone managing their own money, the gap between a 3.4% headline and a 2.5% core is the tell that energy, rather than the broad economy, is what is moving the number.
NBC News preview · Morningstar
Markets Aug 11

Wall Street eased back for a second day as the Hormuz standoff held crude up

The Dow lost 184.13 points, or 0.34%, to close at 53,791.85. The S&P 500 fell 0.32% to 7,728.20 and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 0.60% to 26,445.45. Traders pulled back from risk ahead of the inflation report while the absence of a deal to restore energy flows through the Strait of Hormuz kept oil elevated, with Brent around $89 a barrel and US crude settling near $83. Iran restated that the waterway stays shut until its demands are met.

At the pump the national average for regular was $4.06 on August 6, down three cents on the week and about 8.5% below May's $4.48 peak, but still roughly 30% higher than a year ago. Seasonal demand normally softens from here as school terms begin.

Why it matters: Roughly a quarter of seaborne crude and a fifth of LNG moved through the Strait in 2025, so its status is the single largest swing factor in near-term US fuel costs. For any business that runs vehicles — trades, delivery, mobile services — fuel is now a line item worth repricing rather than absorbing.

◆ The courts

Ruling Aug 11

More than 3,000 social-media addiction lawsuits will proceed against four platforms

The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals rejected Meta's bid to cut off the consolidated litigation, clearing the way for over 3,000 suits against Meta, Google's YouTube, ByteDance's TikTok and Snap to move forward. The court did not rule on the merits of the Section 230 argument. It held that the appeal came too early, reasoning that Section 230 provides a defense against liability rather than immunity from being sued, which is what would have justified an immediate appeal.

The plaintiffs include states, municipalities, school districts and individuals alleging that engagement-optimized design and weak guardrails contributed to depression, anxiety and body-image harm among young users. In the first bellwether trial in March, a Los Angeles jury awarded a 20-year-old plaintiff $6 million and found Meta and Google negligent.

Why it matters: The narrow procedural holding still has a wide effect: it removes the shortcut platforms have used to end design-liability cases before discovery. Thousands of claims now advance toward trials where internal design and research documents get produced, and school districts are among the plaintiffs.

◆ Disaster and response

Wildfire Aug 9–11

Oregon and Washington passed three million acres burned, the most since 2000

The two states crossed three million acres on August 9, with more than 150,000 acres added in a single day. As of Tuesday roughly 51 large fires were burning across both states and suppression costs were estimated at $820 million. Oregon alone has burned nearly 2.3 million acres this year, an area larger than Delaware.

The active fires: Big Grass on the Oregon-Idaho border at 555,147 acres and 35% contained; Wrights Spring in Klamath County at 116,956 acres and 2% contained, with 555 firefighters working to keep it from Chiloquin and Sprague River; and the Grasshopper Fire near Mount Hood at 63,659 acres and 23% contained, where Wasco County expanded evacuation orders Monday night. Clackamas County issued a Level 3 "Go Now" order around the 710-acre Austin Fire, which was 0% contained Tuesday morning.

Why it matters: Suppression spending at this scale draws down state and federal fire budgets before the season's usual peak in late August and September. Smoke from these fires drives air-quality alerts far beyond the Northwest, and $820 million in emergency spending is money that comes out of other line items later.

National Aug 11

A Marine veteran held in Russia for 1,666 days landed at Joint Base Andrews

Robert Gilman, 32, a Massachusetts teacher and Marine veteran, was released from Russian custody Tuesday and arrived at Joint Base Andrews in Maryland that evening. He was detained in 2022 on charges of assaulting a police officer after being removed from a train, charges his family and US officials dispute. He spent 1,666 days in custody and was reported to be in a catatonic state before release. No exchange took place and Russia asked for no one in return. Gilman is being taken to a military hospital in Texas for medical and psychological assessment.

Why it matters: A US citizen came home after more than four years of detention, with no prisoner traded for him. His condition on arrival is now the subject of the medical evaluation in Texas.

◆ Science and sky

Science Aug 12

The only total solar eclipse of 2026 crosses the Northern Hemisphere today

Totality tracks over the Arctic Ocean, Greenland, Iceland and northern Spain, where the sun sits only a few degrees above the horizon and observers get at most about 1 minute 50 seconds. No part of North America sees totality.

New England and parts of the northeastern US get a partial eclipse beginning around midday and running into the mid-afternoon, deeper the farther northeast you go. Atlantic Canada sees roughly half the sun covered at peak. Standard eye protection rules apply for the entire partial phase.

Why it matters: The next total solar eclipse with a path over the contiguous United States is years out, so today's event is a partial one for American viewers. Charlotte and the Southeast are outside the visible partial zone.
