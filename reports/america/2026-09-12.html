◆ The economy

Data September 11

August CPI rose 0.4%, putting annual inflation at 3.4%

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that consumer prices rose 0.4% in August, leaving the 12-month increase at 3.4%. Both figures were in line with estimates. Core CPI, which strips out food and energy, rose 0.3% for the month, slightly above expectations, for an annual rate of 2.4%.

Gasoline drove the headline. Pump prices rose 3.9% in August after a 2.9% decline in July and stand 27.4% above a year earlier, accounting for more than a third of the monthly all-items increase. Shelter, the largest single component, rose 0.3% after two months of 0.1% gains, and its annual rate eased to 3.0% from 3.2% in July.

Why it matters: This was the last inflation reading before the Fed's decision. The composition matters as much as the number: energy is doing the lifting while housing costs cool, which is why the report did not settle the argument about where underlying inflation actually sits.
CNBC — August CPI report · BLS Consumer Price Index Summary
Markets September 11

Stocks snapped a four-session slide as oil pulled back

The Dow closed up 0.98% at 52,573, the S&P 500 rose 0.86% to 7,657 and the Nasdaq gained 0.96% to 26,333, ending a four-day losing run. Breadth was broad, with all 11 S&P sectors higher and technology leading. Dell rose about 10% and Hewlett Packard Enterprise more than 9% as buyers returned to AI hardware.

The 10-year Treasury yield traded around 4.974%, near the three-year high it set earlier in the week, so the rally came without any relief in long rates. The week as a whole was still negative for the major indexes.

Why it matters: For anyone managing their own money, Friday showed equities and bonds reading the same data differently. Stocks priced a softer oil path; the long end priced a Fed that has to move. One of those is going to be repriced next week.
Yahoo Finance — market close, September 11 · The Motley Fool
Ahead September 15–16

The Fed announces Wednesday with a hike priced above 85%

The Federal Open Market Committee meets Tuesday and Wednesday, with the decision at 2:00 p.m. ET on September 16 and a press conference half an hour later. The federal funds target range has been 3.50%–3.75% since December 2025 and was left unchanged at the July 28–29 meeting.

Traders moved further toward a quarter-point increase after the CPI release, with implied odds running above 85%. A move would put the range at 3.75%–4.00%.

Why it matters: A hike would raise the cost of every variable-rate business loan, credit line and card balance in the country within a billing cycle or two, and it would keep mortgage rates elevated in a housing market where existing-home sales have already fallen below 4 million a year.

◆ Energy and the pump

Energy September 7–11

Crude paused near $104 after a 9% week, with talks set for Monday

Brent settled around $104.68 on Friday, down roughly 2.7% on the day, after climbing past $107 earlier in the week to its highest level in nearly four months. The weekly gain was about 9%. The pullback followed Iranian state media reporting that Tehran will meet Gulf states in Oman to discuss shipping through the Strait of Hormuz; Gulf Cooperation Council diplomats are expected to meet their Iranian counterpart on Monday about a temporary arrangement for the waterway.

The run-up has already reached drivers. Gasoline set a Labor Day record at $4.15 a gallon on Monday, and August pump prices were 27.4% above a year earlier. Forecasters disagree on where this goes: the EIA raised its 2027 U.S. crude production forecast to 14.3 million barrels a day, while the IEA sharply cut its global demand outlook.

Why it matters: Fuel is the single largest swing cost for any business that runs vehicles, and it is currently the main thing keeping headline inflation above 3%. Monday's meeting is the next concrete event that could move it in either direction.
CNBC — oil prices and the Strait of Hormuz · EIA Short-Term Energy Outlook

◆ Weather and the land

Climate September 9

NOAA: the hottest U.S. summer in 132 years of records, and drought is spreading

NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information reported that June through August 2026 was the warmest meteorological summer on record for the contiguous United States, exceeding the previous records set in 1936 and 2021 by 0.4°F. August averaged 75.6°F, the warmest August in the 1895-to-present record, with average daytime highs of 88.6°F, also a record. California, Nevada, New Mexico and Colorado each set state records for their warmest August.

Drought coverage followed. As of September 1, 59.1% of the contiguous U.S. was in drought, an increase of more than 10 percentage points since early August. The 2026 wildfire season has burned more than 8.2 million acres across some 52,000 fires.

Why it matters: Drought at this coverage feeds through to crop yields, water restrictions, utility costs and insurance pricing over the following year, and it keeps fire risk elevated across the West well into the fall.
