◆ Markets & money

Markets Week of June 15–19, 2026

A holiday-shortened week ends green as chips power past the Fed scare

Over a four-day week — the NYSE and Nasdaq were closed Friday, June 19, for Juneteenth — the S&P 500 gained 0.9% for its 11th winning week in 12, the Nasdaq jumped 2.4%, and the Dow added 0.7%. The path was bumpy: stocks initially sold off hard after the Fed meeting (the worst "Fed day" reaction for a new chair since 1994), then recovered on semiconductor strength and optimism around the Middle East situation, with the S&P closing up about 1% Thursday.

The backdrop hasn't softened: with April inflation readings running hot (headline PCE near 3.8% year-over-year), markets are no longer pricing a rate cut this year and assign some odds to a hike by December or early 2027.

Why it matters: "Higher for longer" is now the working assumption, not a scare. For a small business, that means borrowing stays expensive and customers stay price-sensitive — fund growth from cash flow, not credit, and lead with offers that save a client money rather than add cost.

◆ Energy & prices

Energy June 19, 2026

Oil sticks near $80 as Hormuz traffic stays slow and talks slip

Crude stayed volatile around the $80 line: Brent traded near $80 a barrel and WTI around $77–78 after a U.S.-Iran meeting in Switzerland was abruptly postponed on June 19 over unresolved logistics. Shipping through the Strait of Hormuz — a chokepoint for a large share of seaborne oil — remained slow, keeping a risk premium in prices even as traders bet on an eventual return to normal flows.

Why it matters: Energy is the single biggest reason inflation stayed sticky into spring, and it feeds straight into fuel, shipping, and input costs for every local business. Until Hormuz traffic normalizes, expect prices at the pump and on freight to stay jumpy — worth a line in any client conversation about cost pressure.

◆ Policy & the grid

Policy June 18, 2026

FERC orders six grid operators to rework how big power users connect

In a unanimous 5-0 vote on June 18, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission issued tailored "show-cause" orders to PJM, MISO, SPP, CAISO, ISO New England, and NYISO, giving them 60 days to justify or revise the rules governing how large loads — AI data centers, manufacturing plants, and other users above roughly 20 megawatts — connect to the transmission system. The orders affect regions serving about 200 million Americans across 30-plus states; Texas, on its own grid, is outside FERC's jurisdiction. The agency framed the move as speeding integration of large loads while protecting existing customers from cost shifts and reliability risk.

Why it matters: Data-center demand is colliding with the grid, and how that load gets connected — and who pays for it — will shape household and small-business electricity bills for years. This is the rare infrastructure story whose outcome shows up directly on a monthly utility statement.
