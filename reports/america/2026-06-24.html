◆ Policy with a concrete effect

National House passed June 23

The "21st Century Road to Housing Act" heads to the President's desk

The House passed the housing package 358–32, one day after the Senate approved it with similarly lopsided bipartisan support; it now goes to the President for signature. The bill runs to more than 50 provisions aimed squarely at housing supply and the cost of building. Among the concrete measures: incentives for local governments to ease zoning and permitting; a grant program funding "pattern books" of pre-approved home designs so builders need fewer custom approvals; removal of the federal rule requiring manufactured homes to keep a permanent chassis, which sponsors say could cut $5,000–$10,000 per home; new limits on how many single-family homes large institutional investors can own; and a program to widen access to small-dollar mortgages.

Why it matters: Housing affordability touches nearly every household and small business. The bill works mostly by trying to increase supply and lower per-unit build costs rather than by subsidizing demand, and it pushes decisions toward the local level — so the real-world effect will depend on how cities and counties respond. For fast-growing markets like the Charlotte metro, the zoning, manufactured-home, and small-dollar-mortgage provisions are the ones most likely to be felt on the ground.

◆ Markets & the economy

Markets Tuesday, June 23 close

A tech-led selloff drags the Nasdaq down 2.2%; semiconductors hit hardest

U.S. stocks fell Tuesday in a global rout of technology shares. The Nasdaq closed down about 2.2% and the S&P 500 fell roughly 1.4%, while the Dow — lighter on tech — finished near flat. Semiconductors were the clear laggard, with the S&P 500 chip group down around 6% on the day, pressured by a sell note flagging higher-rate risk and a tumble in Asian markets. The 10-year Treasury yield sat near 4.5%, the 2-year around 4.19%.

Why it matters: The concentration cuts both ways — the same handful of large tech and chip names that carried the indexes higher can pull them down fast on a single rate scare. For anyone managing their own money, the day was a reminder of how much index performance now rides on a narrow set of stocks, and how sensitive they are to the rate path.

Data ahead Due Thursday, June 25

May PCE inflation lands Thursday — the number the Fed watches most

The May reading of PCE, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, is out Thursday alongside the final estimate of Q1 GDP. Wells Fargo economists look for headline PCE up about 0.5% on the month — pushed by energy — taking the annual rate near 4.1%, with core PCE up about 0.3%. After last week's hawkish Fed meeting, market expectations for the next rate move have shifted toward a possible increase as soon as October, with inflation described as sticky.

Why it matters: A hot PCE print would reinforce the case that rates stay higher for longer, which feeds straight into mortgage rates, small-business borrowing costs, and the housing math the new bill is trying to address. A cooler-than-expected number would do the opposite. It's the single most market-moving data point of the week.
