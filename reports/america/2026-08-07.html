The July jobs report landed this morning as a referendum on whether the Fed raises rates in September

The Bureau of Labor Statistics published the July employment situation at 8:30 a.m. Eastern. It arrived nine days after the FOMC voted 9-3 to hold the federal funds rate at 3.50% to 3.75%, with three regional presidents dissenting in favor of raising it, and after a week in which market-implied odds of a September hike swung between 36% and 68%. Consensus going in was a gain of about 83,000 jobs with unemployment holding at 4.2%.

◆ The economy

Data August 7, 8:30 a.m. ET

The July employment report was scheduled as the first hard read since a split Fed held rates

The setup: on July 29 the FOMC kept the federal funds target at 3.50% to 3.75% on a 9-3 vote. The three dissents came from Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis' Neel Kashkari and Dallas' Lorie Logan, each preferring a 25 basis point increase. That is the largest hawkish dissent count since the early part of the 2022-23 tightening cycle. The committee is balancing a labor market near maximum employment against core inflation still above 2% and added pressure from energy prices.

Market pricing moved all week on Fed commentary rather than data. Traders put the odds of a September increase at 36% before Chair Kevin Warsh spoke, 56% after, 68% by Monday and roughly 58% on Thursday. Forecasters set the bar clearly: a print above 100,000 would likely push hike odds past 70%, while a print under 60,000 would pull them back toward the low 40s. Economists polled by Dow Jones expected 83,000 and a steady 4.2% unemployment rate, with average hourly earnings up 0.3% on the month.

Filing note: the BLS release went out at 8:30 a.m. Eastern this morning. No datable source carrying the actual headline figures was available at the time this briefing was written, so the numbers above are the forecasts and the prior readings, not the print. Check the BLS release directly before acting on any of it.

Why it matters: The direction of the next Fed move decides the cost of a business line of credit, a mortgage, and every variable-rate balance in the country. A US labor market that is holding its unemployment rate up mainly because people are leaving the workforce is a different economy than one adding 200,000 jobs a month, even when the headline rate looks identical.

Markets Week of August 3

Records early in the week, then a pullback, with oil doing most of the driving

The Dow closed above 54,000 for the first time on August 4 and the S&P 500 set a record, helped by falling crude. On August 5 the pattern reversed: the Dow fell 464 points, or 0.85%, to 53,885, ending a five-day winning streak as oil prices pressured stocks. By August 7 the S&P was around 7,704, roughly flat on the session.

Crude itself has been the swing factor. WTI traded at $75.00 and Brent at $79.46 on Thursday after three straight down sessions, on reports that Iran and Oman have agreed on a temporary shipping route through the Strait of Hormuz. A joint statement is in final drafting. Officials on both sides emphasise the route is not a full reopening and is expected to operate for two to four months.

Why it matters: Cheaper energy pulls headline inflation down, which is the argument against a September rate increase. A two-to-four-month arrangement in a waterway that carries a large share of seaborne crude is a temporary fix, so the relief in fuel costs it is producing should be treated as temporary too.

◆ National

Ruling August 6

New Mexico court orders Meta to pay $567 million and cap teen account usage

Chief District Court Judge Bryan Biedscheid issued the remedy in Santa Fe after a two-phase trial in a case brought by state Attorney General Raúl Torrez. The payment is on top of $375 million in civil penalties from the phase-one jury verdict, which found 75,000 violations of the New Mexico Unfair Practices Act. Of the $567 million, $420 million funds treatment services for young people; the rest covers awareness, prevention and screening over five years.

The order also changes the product for minors in New Mexico: a hard cap of 90 hours a month on Facebook and Instagram for under-18 accounts, push notifications paused from 10 p.m. to 7 a.m. daily and from 8 a.m. to 3 p.m. on academic weekdays, tighter limits on adults contacting minors, safeguards around AI chatbots, and expanded review of child sexual abuse reports.

Why it matters: Courts have fined technology companies before. Ordering specific limits on how long a product may be used is a different remedy, and other state attorneys general with similar suits pending now have a template to point at.

Wildfire Through August 7

The national fire year is running well ahead of average, with Spokane still the worst of it

Through July, 42,651 fires had burned more than 4.5 million acres across the country: 130% of the ten-year average by count and 146% by acreage. Washington's Commissioner of Public Lands noted the state was already having one of its busiest fire years on record with August only starting.

The Spokane Complex remains the costliest single event. More than 600 structures were destroyed and tens of thousands of residents evacuated in early August, putting it near the top of Washington's most destructive fires. Governor Bob Ferguson issued a statewide wildfire emergency and burn ban. Spokane authorities arrested a suspect in connection with starting the Old Trails Fire on August 4.

Why it matters: Structure losses at this scale feed directly into property insurance pricing and availability across the Western states, and the season has roughly two months left to run.

◆ Science and space

Research August 6

A model trained on DNA designed 16 viruses that do not exist in nature, and they work

Researchers at the Arc Institute in Palo Alto, with a Stanford-led team, trained a model called Evo on genetic sequences from animals, plants, viruses and microbes, then had it generate viral genomes. Sixteen of the designs proved to be viable bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria. None can infect humans or animals.

The stated aim is phage therapy, vaccine work and drug development. Biosecurity researchers responded the same day by calling for stronger safeguards, on the grounds that the method itself is what matters rather than this particular output.

Why it matters: This is the first time generative AI has produced a functioning organism that had no natural template. It moves the technology's risk and benefit conversation out of software and into biology, where existing oversight was built around human laboratories.
