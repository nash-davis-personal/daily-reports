◆ Economy & markets

Economy Jul 16, 2026

June inflation cooled on both sides and the Fed stayed on hold

The June consumer price index showed inflation slowing for households, and the producer price index fell 0.3% — softer than expected — showing it easing for businesses too. Retail sales rose 0.2% from May, a modest gain. The Federal Reserve held its target range at 3.50–3.75%, and new Chair Kevin Warsh has dropped traditional forward guidance in favor of pure data dependence. Treasury yields ticked up, with the 2-year at 4.158% and the 30-year at 5.107%.

Why it matters: Cooling inflation with a still-resilient consumer is the mix that opens the door to rate cuts later in the year — but the Fed dropping forward guidance means markets get less warning and more volatility around each data release. For anyone managing their own money, borrowing costs and mortgage/credit rates hinge on how the late-July meeting reads this data.

Trade Jul 16, 2026

A 25% tariff on most Brazilian imports takes effect July 22

The US will impose a 25% tariff on most imports from Brazil starting July 22, under Section 301 of the Trade Act, citing trade-practice concerns. Exemptions include beef, orange juice, aircraft and parts, and energy products. A separate US probe into forced-labor enforcement could add another 12.5% duty, with a decision expected next week. Brazil's government has rejected the move and said it will pursue countermeasures.

Why it matters: US businesses importing covered Brazilian goods face a 25% cost increase in under a week, which can flow through to prices on affected categories. The carve-outs for beef, orange juice and energy blunt some consumer impact, but importers with Brazilian supply lines should check their product codes against the exemption list now.

◆ Weather across the country

Disaster Jul 16, 2026

Catastrophic flooding hits Texas again as wildfire smoke fouls the air for 100M+

In Texas, the Guadalupe River rose above the height of a two-story house in about five hours Thursday, triggering dozens of high-water rescues, washing out roads and killing at least one person. At the same time, massive plumes of Canadian wildfire smoke brought dangerous air quality to more than 100 million people across the Midwest and Northeast, layered over extreme East Coast heat. Forecasters describe these simultaneous events as "compound" hazards, and their handling has been complicated by federal cuts to disaster work.

Why it matters: This directly affects a large share of the country right now — air-quality warnings across major metros and active flood danger in Texas. If you're in an affected area, check local air-quality and flood alerts; the overlap of hazards is stretching emergency response thin.
