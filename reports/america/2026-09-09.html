◆ Energy and markets

Breaking September 8–9

Brent passed $100 after strikes on Saudi energy facilities

Attacks attributed to Yemen's Houthi movement struck energy installations in southern Saudi Arabia near the Yemeni border, with Jizan — home to a 400,000 barrel-per-day refinery — among the sites affected. More than 70 people were wounded. The most heavily traded Brent contract settled at $97.89 on Tuesday and rose 2.4% to $100.29 early Wednesday, the first move above $100 in nearly six weeks. West Texas Intermediate futures climbed for a sixth consecutive session.

The supply picture behind the move: most shipping through the Strait of Hormuz remains halted, a waterway that carried about a fifth of the world's oil before the current conflict. Separately, the US military reported striking Iranian tankers in response to attempted missile attacks on a Navy warship.

Why it matters: Crude above $100 works its way into pump prices within roughly two to four weeks and into freight, plastics and food costs after that. For a household, the immediate exposure is fuel; for a small business, it is delivery surcharges and any input priced off petroleum. It also lands directly on the inflation prints due Thursday and Friday, though energy moves this recent will mostly show up in the September data rather than August's.

Markets September 8

Stocks fell in the first session after the holiday weekend

The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed down 628.18 points, or 1.18%, at 52,786.07. The S&P 500 fell 0.58% to 7,673.52 and the Nasdaq Composite fell 0.32% to 26,421.41. Volatility picked up: the VIX closed at 15.72 after finishing the prior session at 14.53. The Dow's underperformance points at the industrial and consumer names most exposed to fuel costs rather than at technology.

Two forces were named in the day's reporting: rising crude, and the 10-year Treasury yield holding near 4.8% and multi-year highs. Trade friction with Canada, whose counter-tariffs on more than 700 US products took effect September 8, was also cited among the session's headwinds.

Why it matters: A day where the Dow falls twice as hard as the Nasdaq is an energy-and-rates story rather than a technology story. For anyone managing their own money, the useful observation is that the decline came with a modest volatility bid — a repricing rather than a panic. The 10-year near 4.8% continues to set the floor under mortgage rates and small-business borrowing costs.

Data September 8

Consumers held their inflation view and lost confidence in the job market

The New York Fed released its August Survey of Consumer Expectations on September 8. Median expected inflation was unchanged at 3.6% one year out and 3.0% five years out, and eased 0.1 point to 3.2% at the three-year horizon. Respondents expected higher gasoline prices over the coming year.

The labor readings moved the other way. The share of respondents expecting a higher unemployment rate a year from now rose to 44.4%, the highest reading since April 2020. That sits alongside an August payrolls report of 162,000 jobs against expectations near 56,000, with prior months revised up and average hourly earnings up 0.3% on the month and 3.1% on the year.

Why it matters: Households expecting a weaker job market while a strong payrolls print lands is the central tension going into next week's Fed meeting. Futures continue to price a meaningful chance of a rate increase on September 16, a direction that would be unusual against consumer sentiment this cautious. August PPI arrives September 10 and August CPI September 11 at 8:30 a.m. ET; both land before the committee meets.
The macro backdrop: Regime reads sideways at medium conviction under "Sovereign-supply regime, policy book re-armed," with the nearest alert-grade wire being the 10-year holding above the 4.75% line at 4.78; note one divergence worth watching — the board's equity-breadth restore requires two consecutive S&P closes at or above 7,680, and Tuesday's 7,673.52 close sits just under it. The board carries no forward-dated catalyst past its September 6 weekly pass, so the calendar items above are the near-term markers. ◆ Science, space and weather

Space September 8–12

Crew-13 is targeting September 12, with a Space Force launch two days earlier

NASA and SpaceX are working toward a Crew-13 launch to the International Space Station as soon as September 12, carrying two American astronauts, one Canadian and one Russian. Ahead of it, a Falcon 9 is scheduled to lift the classified USSF-153 payload for the US Space Force from Space Launch Complex 4 East at Vandenberg on September 10, with a three-hour window opening at 8:37 a.m. Pacific.

The pace follows the August 30 launch of NASA's $4.3 billion Roman Space Telescope, whose boosters recovered at Landing Zone 2 and Landing Zone 40.

Why it matters: Crew rotations to the station are the most visible measure of whether US human spaceflight is running on schedule, and this one is on cadence. The Roman telescope, now on station beyond Earth, begins a survey mission whose science output will run for years.

Weather September 8

The Atlantic basin is empty at the peak of hurricane season

As of September 8 there were no tropical cyclones in the Atlantic, at the point in the calendar when activity normally peaks. The 2026 season has produced two named storms — Arthur and Bertha — and no hurricanes to date. NOAA has kept its below-normal outlook of 7 to 13 named storms, 2 to 6 hurricanes and 0 to 2 major hurricanes. Several tropical waves are crossing the Atlantic with scattered convection but none organized.

In the Central Pacific, Lowell continued weakening after passing west of Kauai, with maximum sustained winds down near 80 mph and further weakening forecast.

Why it matters: A quiet peak reduces the near-term risk of a storm-driven disruption to Gulf refining capacity, which matters more than usual with crude already above $100. Roughly half the season remains, so the outlook is a probability rather than an all-clear.
