◆ The economy

Markets Oct 16 close

Chip and software stocks drag the Nasdaq to its worst week since June

The Nasdaq Composite fell 2.14% to 26,140.03, the S&P 500 dropped 1.09% to 7,659.44 and the Dow slipped 0.34% to 52,002.41. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 3.83%, its eighth straight lower close, a streak coverage called the longest since 2008; it lost roughly 10% on the week. The Nasdaq-100 fell about 5.5% for the week, ending a four-week winning streak. AMD, Intel and Micron each fell more than 5%, software stocks fell 4.8% as a group, and AI-infrastructure names were hit hardest: CoreWeave fell 11.9% to $61.39, down 25% on the week. Six AI-heavy mega caps made up more than half the S&P's drop; the equal-weight S&P fell only 0.4%. Energy stocks and insurers rose.

Coverage cited worries about Chinese chip competition, debt-funded AI data-center spending and higher oil prices feeding the inflation outlook.

Why it matters: The sell-off was narrow. Most of the damage sat in a handful of large AI and chip names that now make up a big share of index funds, so a plain S&P 500 fund holder felt more of it than the average stock did. For anyone managing their own money, concentration in a few names is the thing to check, more than the index level.

Energy Oct 16

Brent touches $110 and U.S. crude settles above $100

Brent rose 3.7% to $109.86 a barrel after briefly touching $110, its highest since May. U.S. benchmark WTI settled at $101.41 after a midday surge, and diesel recovered all of the prior week's losses. Market coverage pointed to supply risks: strikes on Russian refineries and energy assets, attacks on shipping and infrastructure around the Strait of Hormuz and Saudi Arabia, and uncertainty over whether Congress blocks a plan to release Russian diesel.

Why it matters: September CPI already showed gasoline up 9.1%. Crude above $100 tends to reach pump prices within a couple of weeks, and diesel hits delivery, trucking and anything with a fuel surcharge, which is most small businesses' supply costs.

The Fed Oct 15–17

Warsh gives no guidance as the Fed enters its pre-meeting blackout

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, speaking in Bangkok, said the Fed had "removed a dose of accommodation" and offered nothing on next steps; at the IMF a day earlier he said he doesn't prejudge meetings. The blackout began Saturday ahead of the October 27–28 meeting. Futures put the odds of an October rate hike near 18–20% and a December hike near 90%. Cleveland Fed President Hammack said financial conditions don't look restrictive, and Chicago's Goolsbee called September "discouraging" on inflation. The 10-year Treasury yield held at 5.09% through the stock sell-off. Freddie Mac's latest 30-year mortgage average was 7.40%.

Why it matters: Borrowing costs aren't coming down soon. With markets leaning toward a hike by December, anyone planning a loan, refinance or big financed purchase should price it at today's rates or higher.

◆ Industry and infrastructure

Investment Oct 16

Taiwan plans to fund U.S. industrial parks, starting with a $4B Texas complex

Bloomberg reports Taiwan plans to finance U.S. industrial parks for suppliers to Nvidia, TSMC and Foxconn, starting with a Texas complex costing up to $4 billion. It follows Japan's plan, reported this week, to invest up to $21 billion in two New York chip factories.

Why it matters: Foreign money keeps flowing into U.S. chip supply chains even in a week when chip stocks fell hard. These projects mean construction, utility and service work in the host regions for years.
