◆ The Northwest fire season

Disaster August 9

Three million acres across two states, and a fire crossing a river into new evacuation zones

Oregon and Washington have now burned more than three million acres between them this season, with 52 active large fires still going. Reporting describes it as the highest combined acreage since 2000. Oregon on its own broke the record it set in 2024. The rate is the alarming part: more than 150,000 additional acres burned on Saturday, roughly double what went on Friday.

The Hagen Fire crossed to the north side of the North Fork Umatilla River, which pushed Umatilla County to raise evacuation levels again, with new Level 3 "Go Now" zones and expanded Level 2 "Be Ready" areas across northeast Oregon. Highway 204 is closed between Weston and Elgin, mileposts 0 to 39. The Oregon Air National Guard worked a second day on the Grasshopper Fire, shifting from structure protection to mop-up and patrolling fire line. A new fire was reported along Forest Road 46 in the upper Clackamas River drainage under Mt. Hood National Forest. Nationally, the interagency center still has the country at Preparedness Level 5, with 94 uncontained large fires and 6,328,055 acres burned since January 1.

Why it matters: Preparedness Level 5 means national firefighting resources are committed and any new large fire competes for crews and aircraft that are already assigned. Evacuation levels in northeast Oregon are moving upward day to day rather than settling, and Highway 204's closure cuts a route through the Blue Mountains. Smoke from fires this size routinely degrades air quality far outside the burn areas.

◆ A record market going into an inflation week

Markets August 9

Payrolls fell by 23,000 and stocks had their best week since April

The July employment report showed nonfarm payrolls declining by 23,000 against a forecast of an 80,000 gain. Equities read the miss as removing the case for another rate increase and rallied. The S&P 500 closed Friday at 7,757.64, up 0.62% on the day and 3.58% on the week, at a record. The Nasdaq Composite finished at 26,690.62, up 5.19% on the week, and the Dow at 54,036.93, up 2.96%. The 10-year Treasury yield eased to about 4.64% from a high near 4.74%. Futures moved the probability of a September rate increase down to roughly 44% from 67% a week earlier.

This week decides whether that repricing holds. Existing home sales come Tuesday. July CPI publishes Wednesday, August 12 at 8:30 a.m., with consensus at 0.1% month over month and 3.4% year over year — worth noting because yesterday's edition put CPI on Tuesday, and the BLS calendar has it on Wednesday. PPI follows Thursday and retail sales Friday. Cisco reports Wednesday and Applied Materials Thursday, which puts AI infrastructure demand into the same week as the macro data.

Why it matters: The debate is over whether the Fed raises in September, with inflation running near 3.5% while hiring contracts. A hot CPI on Wednesday reopens the case for an increase and would unwind a good part of last week's rally; a soft one supports a hold. For anyone with a mortgage rate, a variable business loan or a savings balance in play, Wednesday at 8:30 is the moment that matters this week.

◆ Where the AI buildout meets the county commission

Policy August 9

More than 500 local governments have now restricted data centers, and two states went statewide

The Information reports that over 500 US towns and counties have passed bans or restrictions on data centers, up from more than 300 in late June, with New York and Texas adding statewide limits. Tracking by the Rockefeller Institute counted more than 533 moratorium instruments across 42 states as of July, with over 150 passed in July alone. New York's measure, the Responsible Data Center Development Act, imposes a one-year moratorium on new permits for facilities above 20 megawatts and adds separate utility rate classes and impact studies.

The pattern is local and fast. Objections center on electricity demand, water use, property tax abatements and noise, and they are being resolved at the zoning-board level rather than in Washington. In the same week, Meta announced a $1 billion fund for US communities near its data centers.

Why it matters: The physical expansion of AI now runs through thousands of separate local approvals, which is slower and less predictable than the capital behind it assumed. For residents in areas being scouted for sites, the decisions that determine whether one gets built are happening at county and town meetings. For electricity customers, the rate-class question New York wrote into its law is the one to follow, since it determines whether data center power costs land on other ratepayers.

◆ Cost of living

Housing August 10

San Francisco rent passed New York's, at $3,728 a month

Average rent in San Francisco has risen 18% in under two years to $3,728 a month, moving above New York City, according to the Wall Street Journal. The reporting attributes the increase to competition for housing from AI company salaries. The city spent much of the post-2020 period as the example of an emptied-out downtown, and the reversal has taken less than two years.

Why it matters: It is a concrete measure of how concentrated the AI employment boom is geographically, and of who absorbs the cost. Renters in San Francisco without AI-sector wages are competing against a payroll they are not on. The same dynamic shows up in smaller form in any metro that lands a large tech employer.
