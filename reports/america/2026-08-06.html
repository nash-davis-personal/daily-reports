◆ Disaster

Wildfire August 2–6

Fires around Spokane destroyed hundreds of structures and emptied half the city's neighborhoods

Three fires — Old Trails, Autumn Lane and Fairview — are burning in and around Spokane, a city of more than 230,000 and Washington's second-largest. Combined they have covered more than 7,000 acres and destroyed roughly 700 structures, with about 65,000 people evacuated. Spokane County Fire District 3 Chief Cody Rohrbach attributed 640 of the destroyed structures to the Old Trails fire alone, which has engulfed about 12 square miles and remains uncontained. Officials have not yet broken the losses down into homes, businesses and schools.

Mayor Lisa Brown said nearly half the city's neighborhoods have either been evacuated or are at risk. Governor Bob Ferguson requested emergency support from the federal government and FEMA. The fires are burning into a broader pattern across the West: critical fire weather in the Northwest driven by strong winds and very dry air, and a heat dome over the Southwest pushing temperatures well above 110°F.

Why it matters: A fire that takes 640 structures in one run is a housing and insurance event for a mid-sized metro, not just an emergency-response one. Rebuild demand, displaced residents and insurer exposure in the inland Northwest will persist long after containment, and the federal disaster request sets the timeline for how quickly any of it gets funded.

◆ The economy

Jobs August 5

Private payrolls added 44,000 in July, the smallest gain since January

ADP's National Employment Report came in at 44,000 against a Dow Jones consensus of 75,000, and June was revised down to 95,000. The composition is narrow: services added 47,000 while goods-producing companies shed 3,000, and education and health services alone accounted for 36,000 of the gain. Annual pay was up 4.4% for workers who stayed put and 7% for those who changed jobs, the largest switcher premium since August 2025.

The Fed held the federal funds target at 3.50%–3.75% at its July 29 meeting. Core PCE has run above the 2% target since March 2021, and the energy shock from the Iran conflict pushed market expectations away from 2026 cuts and toward the possibility of hikes. A soft ADP print argues for patience; Kansas City's Jeff Schmid and Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari pushed back with hawkish remarks the same day. The government's July Employment Situation lands Friday, August 7 at 8:30 a.m. ET, with economists expecting roughly 85,000 to 95,000 payrolls and unemployment steady at 4.2%.

Why it matters: A slow-hire, slow-fire labor market with pay growth above 4% is the specific combination that keeps the Fed still. For anyone managing their own money, the practical read is that the rate path now hinges on Friday's number and the next inflation print rather than on any single Fed speech, and that a weak ADP has repeatedly failed to predict the official figure.

Energy August 4–6

Crude and gasoline fell sharply as a Strait of Hormuz agreement moved closer

September WTI closed down 5.69% on August 4 at a three-week low, and gasoline futures fell 3.86% to their lowest nearest-futures level in about four and three-quarter months. Brent settled below $80 and WTI traded near $76. The move continued into August 6.

The driver is negotiation rather than supply. Iranian and Omani negotiators have finalized a draft deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and are awaiting final approval from Iran's supreme leader. The structure under discussion is an interim 60-day accord with no tolls or fees, inbound vessels using a northern lane and outbound traffic a southern lane. Traders are pricing the supply that returns if the waterway reopens.

Why it matters: Fuel is a direct input cost for every trades business, delivery operation and commuter in the country, and it has been the main channel pushing inflation expectations up this year. A sustained pullback in pump prices does more to loosen the Fed's constraint than any single labor print. The deal is not signed, so the move reverses if approval stalls.

Markets August 5

The S&P 500 and Dow set records while two of the market's biggest AI names fell hard

Both indices hit record highs on August 5 on Middle East de-escalation hopes, absorbing sharp declines in SpaceX and AMD. SpaceX closed down 13.6% at $108.27 in its first quarter as a public company, beating on revenue and adjusted EBITDA but disclosing $15.8 billion of capital spending on AI computing infrastructure and more than $18 billion in total capex for the quarter. AMD fell about 6% to $486.60 despite record revenue of $11.5 billion and adjusted EPS of $1.66, both double beats.

Earlier in the week Amazon crossed $3 trillion in market value, the fifth company to do so, after AWS revenue rose 37% year over year to $42.2 billion and full-year capex guidance moved from $200 billion to $220 billion. The through-line for the week is that beating estimates no longer protects a stock if the capital-spending line keeps rising.

Why it matters: Index records and heavy single-name drawdowns happening on the same day is a breadth story. For a self-directed investor it is a reminder that "the market is at a high" and "my holdings are at a high" are separate statements, and that AI-adjacent names are now being judged on cash conversion rather than on growth alone.

◆ Carolinas

Local August

Two plant and distribution closures take 655 jobs out of the Charlotte region this month

Family Dollar's distribution center in Matthews is eliminating 373 positions, with the closure concluding by mid-August. Nippon Electric Glass is shutting its long-running Shelby manufacturing plant on August 31, displacing another 282 employees. Both sit inside the Charlotte metro's commuting range.

The statewide picture is still growth. North Carolina's real GDP is forecast to rise 2.1% in 2026 with all 15 economic sectors expanding, information leading at 3.9%, and about 80,850 net jobs added statewide. Unemployment is projected to drift up to 4.1% by December from 3.7% a year earlier. Charlotte-area leaders point to more than 7,000 jobs and $1.4 billion of potential investment in the pipeline.

Why it matters: Regional job growth and specific plant closures coexist. The concrete effect is several hundred experienced logistics and manufacturing workers entering the Gaston and Mecklenburg County labor market at once, which loosens hiring for local employers and tightens household budgets in those two towns.
