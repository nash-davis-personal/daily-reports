◆ Rates and markets

Fed July 29

The FOMC held at 3.50–3.75%, and the dissents were all in the hawkish direction

The Committee left the target range for the federal funds rate at 3-1/2 to 3-3/4 percent, its fifth consecutive hold, on a 9–3 vote. All three dissenters were regional bank presidents: Beth Hammack of Cleveland, Neel Kashkari of Minneapolis and Lorie Logan of Dallas. Each preferred raising the range by a quarter percentage point at this meeting.

The statement described price pressures as still above the 2% objective, attributing part of that to supply disruptions that have raised costs in sectors including energy, and described job creation as roughly matching workforce growth with unemployment little changed. Officials' year-end rate projections run between 3.6% and 4.1%, and the Committee has penciled in one quarter-point increase by the end of 2026.

Why it matters: Three dissents in favor of a hike is unusual and it changes the direction of risk for anyone borrowing. The next move priced by markets is up rather than down, which affects variable-rate business credit, SBA loan pricing and mortgage rates. Anyone waiting for cheaper money before financing a purchase is now waiting on a scenario the Fed itself is not forecasting.
FOMC statement CNBC
Markets July 29

Equities snapped a three-day run: Dow off 1,100 points, yields up, oil higher

The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 2.19% to 51,594, the S&P 500 lost 1.52% to close at 7,316.15, and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 1.74% to 24,443. The 10-year Treasury yield rose about 7 basis points to above 4.67% as the market read the hold as hawkish. Crude added to the pressure: Brent traded near $90 a barrel and West Texas Intermediate above $84 after a jump of nearly 8% in the prior session, following renewed US strikes on Iran-linked targets. Tehran has denied that negotiations are taking place.

Why it matters: Two costs move together here. Higher long yields raise the price of credit for equipment and real estate, and crude near $90 feeds into fuel, freight and anything delivered. For an owner-operator the practical exposure is fuel and shipping surcharges showing up in the next billing cycle.

Data July 30

Q2 GDP and June PCE published this morning — the official prints were not retrievable at filing time

The Bureau of Economic Analysis released the advance estimate of second-quarter GDP together with June personal income and outlays, which carries the PCE and core PCE price indexes, at 8:30 a.m. Eastern today. Both were scheduled the morning after the Fed decision, so they will shape the September meeting rather than this one.

Flagging this honestly: the actual figures were not available from a verifiable source when this briefing was written. The setup going in was consensus for about 2.3% annualized Q2 growth against 2.1% in Q1, with the Atlanta Fed's GDPNow tracker at 1.6% as of July 27, and June core PCE expected at 0.2% month over month with the annual rate easing to roughly 3.3% from a three-year high near 3.4%. Headline CPI had already cooled to 3.50% in June from 4.20% in May. Check the numbers directly before treating any of them as fact.

Why it matters: This pair is the input to whether the Fed's penciled-in hike happens in September. Growth near 2% with core inflation still above 3% is the combination that keeps a hike on the table, and it is the reason three officials already voted for one.

◆ Corporate America

Earnings July 29

Meta beat on revenue, missed badly on profit, and raised the floor on its spending

Meta reported $60.8 billion in revenue against $60.2 billion expected, with advertising at $59.3 billion, but earnings per share came in at $6.18 versus the $7.14 consensus. Capital expenditures were $31.08 billion for the quarter, and operating cash flow of $31.86 billion left just $784 million in free cash flow, down from $8.55 billion a year earlier. Full-year 2026 capex guidance narrowed upward to $135–145 billion from $125–145 billion, lifting the low end by $10 billion in a single quarter. Shares fell close to 8% after hours.

Apple and Amazon report after the close today, which puts three of the four largest AI spenders on the record inside 48 hours.

Why it matters: Investors rewarded Microsoft for spending and punished Meta for it. The difference was visible revenue growth attached to the spend. For anyone holding index funds, this is the AI capex trade being repriced company by company rather than as a sector.

Enforcement July 29

The FTC, Utah and Los Angeles County sued Hims & Hers over health data sharing and cancellation practices

The complaint, filed Wednesday in the US District Court for the Northern District of California, alleges the telehealth company shared users' sensitive health information with advertising platforms including Meta and Snap — both by uploading customer lists and through third-party tracking technologies that automatically transmitted certain events — while promising privacy. It also alleges the company began charging for prescriptions before customers met with a provider, and failed to clearly disclose when monthly refills would bill, making cancellation before the next cycle difficult. Hims & Hers rejected the allegations, saying the suit "disregards substantial evidence" from a nearly three-year investigation. The stock fell 10%.

Why it matters: This is the clearest small-business read-through of the week. Two ordinary practices are being treated as violations: letting an ad pixel fire on pages that reveal something sensitive about a customer, and running a recurring charge whose renewal date and cancel path are not obvious. Any business with a subscription and a Meta pixel should check both this week.
FTC press release CNBC

◆ Science & space

Launch July 30

A Falcon 9 put a classified reconnaissance payload in orbit before dawn

SpaceX launched a classified payload for the National Reconnaissance Office at 3:10 a.m. EDT this morning from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, and recovered the booster. Separately, the Starship Version 3 vehicle from Flight Test 13 was still floating off the coast of Western Australia five days after its July 24 splashdown, a sign of how intact the water landing was. NASA's Roman Space Telescope is scheduled to launch on a Falcon Heavy on August 30.

Why it matters: Launch cadence at this level is now routine enough that a national-security payload goes up overnight without much notice. The Roman telescope at the end of August is the next launch with real scientific consequence.
