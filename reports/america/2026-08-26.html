◆ The economy

Data Aug 25, 2026

Confidence slipped to 89.4, and the forward-looking half fell hardest

The Conference Board's consumer confidence index fell 0.8 points to 89.4 in August, from a downwardly revised 90.2 in July. It is the second consecutive monthly decline and the lowest reading in seven months. The Board attributed the move to darkening views of the job market and of inflation.

The split inside the survey is the part worth noting. The Present Situation Index, which measures how consumers rate current business and labor conditions, rose 6.8 points to 121.2 and broke a three-month losing streak. The Expectations Index, covering the short-term outlook for income, business and jobs, fell 5.8 points to 68.2. Within that: 26.1% of consumers expect fewer jobs ahead, up from 25.3%; 17.6% expect their income to rise, down from 19.5%; and 13.8% expect it to fall, up from 12.6%.

Why it matters: People describing current conditions as better while describing the next six months as worse is a pattern that shows up in spending behavior before it shows up in the labor data — households keep buying but pull back on anything deferrable. For a small business, the deferrable purchase is the one you sell.
The Conference Board — US Consumer Confidence
Housing Aug 25, 2026

New single-family home sales fell 10.5% in July

New single-family home sales dropped to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 607,000 in July, down 10.5% from an upwardly revised June pace and the lowest level since January. The Census Bureau report landed the same morning as the confidence survey.

The rate backdrop has not moved in buyers' favor. The average interest rate on a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage is near its highest level in more than a year, with no immediate relief in sight for would-be buyers.

Why it matters: New-home sales feed directly into construction employment, appliance and furniture demand, and the trades that serve new households. A 10.5% single-month drop with mortgage rates at a one-year high points to demand held back by financing cost rather than by preference, which means it can return quickly if rates move — and stays stuck if they do not.

Markets Aug 25, 2026

Stocks closed higher on a semiconductor rebound and softer yields

The S&P 500 rose 0.32% to 7,677.28, the Nasdaq Composite added 0.66% to 26,151.30, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average gained 160.24 points, or 0.3%, to 53,577.40 for a third straight winning session. Companies with AI-infrastructure exposure recovered from the previous session's chip sell-off, and Treasury yields eased across the curve.

The two data releases that morning were both soft, and the market went up regardless. That is the setup heading into Nvidia's results after today's close — guidance is around $91 billion — and Friday's PCE price index, where Barclays expects a 0.2% monthly increase in the core measure. Those two prints decide more about the rest of the week than anything released so far.

Why it matters: For anyone managing their own money, this is a session where the index and the underlying data disagreed. That gap resolves one way or the other on the two scheduled events ahead, and both fall inside the next 72 hours. Positioning decisions made before Friday afternoon are being made without the week's most important information.

◆ Energy

Commodities Aug 25, 2026

Oil fell to a one-week low as traders shrugged off new Iran sanctions

Brent crude futures slid more than 3% to around $89 a barrel and West Texas Intermediate fell by a similar margin to near $82, the lowest in a week. The move followed the newest round of US sanctions targeting Iran, which traders judged less disruptive to physical supply than a military conflict would be. The shift from military confrontation to economic pressure is what calmed the market.

Supply concerns have not gone away. The UAE halted trade and financial transactions with Iran last week, commerce through the Strait of Hormuz — historically about a fifth of seaborne oil — has been constrained, and Iran retains the ability to disrupt shipping, which keeps a residual premium in the price.

Worth flagging as a genuine disagreement rather than smoothing it over: TSD's internal Macroscope board reads the physical transit data as tightening even while the price fell, counting three vessels through Hormuz on August 23 against an August daily mean of 4.9 and a July mean of 10.2. The price tape and the transit count are pointing opposite ways right now, and both are shown here rather than one being picked.

Why it matters: Fuel is a direct input cost for every trade business that drives a truck, and it feeds through to freight and to grocery prices with a lag. A one-week low is relief at the pump if it holds. The reason for the caveat is that the price is reflecting a judgment about intent while the shipping data reflects what is physically moving, and those can stay apart for a while before one of them corrects.

◆ Policy with a concrete effect on small business

Regulation Comment window closes Sept 18, 2026

The FTC has a personalized-pricing enforcement statement out for comment

The Federal Trade Commission opened a 30-day comment period on August 19 on a proposed enforcement policy statement covering personalized pricing — the use of a consumer's own data, such as browsing history, location, demographics or purchase patterns, to estimate what that person will pay and set an individual price. Comments close September 18 at 11:59 PM ET.

The substance is that the FTC intends to apply Section 5 of the FTC Act, which covers deceptive practices, to businesses that set individualized prices without adequate disclosure. The reasoning stated is that consumers generally expect a displayed price to be the same price offered to everyone else for the same product, so a seller who represents or implies a price is static when it in fact varies by individual is at risk. The Commission notes that advances in data analytics and AI have expanded the practical reach of this across consumer markets. It is the most direct federal articulation so far of how existing consumer protection law applies here.

Why it matters: This lands on any business running dynamic or segment-based pricing through a website or an AI tool, which now includes a lot of ordinary e-commerce and booking software that adjusts prices per visitor. The operative question the statement raises is disclosure. It does not propose banning the practice. Anyone whose site prices vary by visitor has a month to read it and decide whether their checkout page says so.
