◆ The strait, and the price of fuel

Breaking Aug 30–31, 2026

Strikes resumed after a month, over an attempted mining of the Strait of Hormuz

US Central Command said American forces struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island on Sunday after Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps forces were observed preparing to launch rockets carrying sea mines into the Strait of Hormuz. CENTCOM spokesman Navy Capt. Tim Hawkins gave the account. It was the first publicly acknowledged US strike on Iranian territory in more than a month; CENTCOM had finished clearing sea mines from the strait's international shipping routes the week before. Iran's Revolutionary Guards acknowledged the strikes, reported casualties and said they would respond. Iranian media said eight missiles were fired at two US bases in Jordan, and Jordan's military said all eight were intercepted.

The conflict is in its sixth month and has sharply reduced vessel traffic through the strait, which before the war carried roughly a fifth of global energy exports. Separately, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the US is beginning weekly secondary sanctions aimed at Iran's trading partners, starting with banks.

Why it matters: Roughly a fifth of the world's energy exports normally move through this waterway, so a mining attempt is a supply event before it is anything else. The concrete American effect shows up at the pump and in diesel-linked freight costs with a lag of a week or two, and in the inflation prints the Fed reads before its next decision.

Markets Aug 31 – Sept 4, 2026

August closes up about 3%, into the year's historically weakest month

Today is August's last session. The S&P 500 is on track to finish the month up close to 3% and rose 0.5% last week, after Friday's decline. Futures were lower early Monday on the renewed US–Iran fighting.

The calendar tightens from tomorrow: July construction spending, the August ISM manufacturing PMI and the July JOLTS report on September 1; August ADP private payrolls and the Federal Reserve Beige Book on September 2; and the August employment report from the Bureau of Labor Statistics at 8:30 a.m. ET on Friday, September 4. For context on the month ahead rather than a forecast: over the past 50 years the S&P 500 has averaged a 0.7% loss in September, with gains in 46% of them.

Why it matters: Friday's payrolls number is the week's decisive input for the Fed's next decision, and July's employment reading had already stalled unexpectedly. For anyone managing their own money, the practical point is that a single Friday morning release carries more weight than the four sessions before it.

Small business Comments due Sept 21, 2026

SBA has proposed the biggest rewrite of small-business size standards in a generation

The Small Business Administration published a proposal on August 20 that would replace close to a thousand industry-specific size standards with 338 standards set at the four- and five-digit NAICS level, remove all subindustry exceptions, convert construction and several other sectors from receipts-based to employee-based thresholds, drop the ceiling on size standards and add a productivity adjustment on top of inflation. Thresholds would rise as much as tenfold or more in professional services, IT, engineering, logistics and hospitality, and SBA proposes not to reduce any standard.

SBA estimates roughly 114,500 firms would gain small-business status and fewer than 200 would lose it. The National Small Business Association has objected that the change would upend small-business access to federal contracts. Comments close on September 21.

Why it matters: Small-business status governs eligibility for set-aside federal contracts and SBA programs. Raising the thresholds widens the pool competing for the same set-asides, which is why the change is contested by firms currently at the small end of it. Anyone whose revenue depends on those programs has three weeks to file a comment.
Federal Register Forbes

◆ Science, and the weather

Space Aug 30, 2026

Roman separated on time and is now commissioning toward L2

NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope lifted off from Kennedy Space Center at 7:26 a.m. Sunday aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy and separated from the second stage on schedule at 7:57 a.m. EDT, 31 minutes after launch. NASA concluded launch coverage the same day. The observatory is roughly the size of a tour bus, cost $4.3 billion, and launched about nine months ahead of its schedule.

It is now deploying solar panels and preparing a mid-course correction burn for L2, about a million miles from Earth on the anti-sun side. Its science program covers dark energy and dark matter, a statistical census of planetary systems, and general astrophysics.

Why it matters: A flagship NASA observatory reaching orbit ahead of schedule and on budget-cycle is uncommon enough to note. Commissioning at L2 takes months, so first science images are not imminent.
NASA NPR
Weather Aug 31, 2026

No Atlantic storms on the board with the peak ten days out

The National Hurricane Center lists no active tropical cyclones in the Atlantic this morning; it is watching a disturbance in the northern Gulf. NOAA has maintained its below-normal seasonal outlook of 7 to 13 named storms, 2 to 6 hurricanes and 0 to 2 major hurricanes, with a 75% chance of below-normal activity and a 5% chance of above-normal.

The Pacific is the active basin. Hurricane Karina was a Category 4 with 145 mph sustained winds well out in the eastern Pacific as of 11 p.m. HST Sunday, moving west, and Tropical Storm Lowell was strengthening on a westward track in the central Pacific at 60 mph. Neither is a threat to the continental United States on its current track.

Why it matters: The Atlantic season climatologically peaks around September 10, and an empty basin this close to it is the quiet part of a season, not the end of one. Coastal and Carolina readers get a clear week; nothing about a below-normal forecast changes what a single landfall would do.
