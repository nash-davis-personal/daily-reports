◆ Public health

Outbreak Aug 9–10

One pepper supplier put salsa and guacamole recalls into five national grocery chains

CDC has counted 345 people infected with the outbreak strain across 27 states since late July, with 36 hospitalizations and no deaths reported. FDA and CDC identified fresh jalapenos distributed by Coast Citrus Distributors as the vehicle and named a grower in Sinaloa, Mexico as the likely source.

Taylor Farms has recalled more than a dozen products made with those peppers — salsas and guacamoles sold at Walmart, Target, Kroger, Whole Foods and Trader Joe's. Recalled items went to retail distribution centers in 26 states, including North Carolina neighbors Tennessee, South Carolina and Georgia, and carry "Best If Used By" dates up to and including August 16, 2026.

Why it matters: The affected date codes are still inside their shelf life, so the exposure window is open right now rather than closed. Anyone with refrigerated salsa or guacamole from those retailers should check the brand and date against the FDA notice before eating it.

◆ Markets and the economy

Markets Aug 10

Stocks gave back a little after their best week since April

The S&P 500 slipped 0.06% to 7,753.11, the Nasdaq Composite fell 0.32% to 26,605.36 and the Dow lost 0.11% to 53,975.98, ending a five-session winning streak that had carried the S&P to a record close. Intel was a notable laggard, down about 4% after announcing a share sale. Nvidia pulled back around 3%.

The setup into Tuesday was mixed futures with oil sharply higher. The week's data run is dense: July CPI Wednesday morning, PPI Thursday, retail sales and the preliminary University of Michigan consumer sentiment reading Friday.

Why it matters: July payrolls came in at minus 23,000 with unemployment at 4.1%, which traders read as clearing the way for the Fed to hold in September rather than hike. Wednesday's CPI either confirms that or reopens the hawkish case, and it is the single print most likely to move rates on a mortgage, a business loan or a savings yield this month.

Energy Aug 10–11

Oil is climbing because the Strait of Hormuz is still closed

Brent rose above $86 a barrel Monday, a fourth consecutive session of gains, with prices tracking whether Iran and Oman finalize an agreement to restore shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. Iran's foreign minister said Tehran is not in direct talks with Washington and has tied reopening to the lifting of the US blockade on Iranian shipping and compensation for damages. US officials have rejected the compensation demand.

WTI settled near $78.20 on Friday after a weekly decline of more than 7%, which shows how fast the price moves on headlines rather than on barrels actually shipped.

Why it matters: Energy feeds directly into the CPI print two days out and into every business with a fleet or a shipping line. A resolution that reopens Hormuz would return millions of barrels a day to the market; continued closure keeps upward pressure on fuel costs through the fall.

◆ Fire season and infrastructure

Disaster Aug 10

Oregon and Washington are past three million acres, the most since 2000

The two-state total has crossed three million acres burned this season. Evacuation levels rose again for the Hagen Fire along Highway 204 in Umatilla County. The Grasshopper Fire in Mount Hood National Forest stood at an estimated 63,659 acres Monday morning. The Rowe Creek Complex has burned 364,413 acres at 76% containment, while the Hay Creek Complex reached 100% containment at roughly 200,292 acres. Wasco County lowered Tygh Valley and Fairgrounds Road from Level 2 to Level 1.

FEMA has authorized federal funds toward firefighting costs for the Hagen Fire in Umatilla and Union counties and the Wrights Spring Fire in Klamath County and on Klamath Tribes land. Air quality remains unhealthy across much of Oregon.

Why it matters: Weeks of fire season remain. Beyond the immediate evacuations, sustained burns of this size drive smoke across state lines, push regional insurance and utility costs up, and pull federal disaster funds that are drawn on nationally.

Investment Aug 10

A Texas bitcoin campus signed a 20-year, $9.1 billion power-and-space deal

Riot Platforms disclosed a 20-year agreement to supply 191 megawatts of data-center capacity at its Rockdale, Texas campus, running through June 2048, with two five-year extensions that would take the total to $16.1 billion. Bloomberg reported the tenant is Anthropic. Delivery is phased, with the first 96MW targeted for December 2027. Riot's shares rose more than 20% after hours.

Separately, Intel priced a $20 billion share sale at $95, upsized from the $15 billion it announced that morning and its first public share sale since listing in 1971, to fund contract-manufacturing capacity.

Why it matters: Both are the same story in different form — capital moving into domestic compute and power capacity at a scale that shows up in local tax bases, construction employment and electricity demand. More than 500 US towns and counties have now placed restrictions on data centers, which makes where this capacity lands a live local question rather than a settled one.
