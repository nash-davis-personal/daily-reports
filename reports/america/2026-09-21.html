◆ The economy and the summit

National September 21–24

Xi arrives for a state visit; the trade truce is the central question

Xi Jinping arrived in Washington for a state visit, his first to the White House in a decade and the two leaders' second meeting this year following a US presidential visit to China in May. A senior US official said the president plans to greet Xi at Joint Base Andrews on Wednesday, followed by a formal arrival ceremony at the White House and a military review in the Rose Garden. The main bilateral meeting is Thursday, with a state dinner that evening; technology executives including Jeff Bezos, Sundar Pichai, Sam Altman, Tim Cook, Elon Musk and Jensen Huang are expected to attend.

The stated agenda is trade, artificial intelligence and critical minerals, with Taiwan, agricultural and aircraft purchases, fentanyl precursor chemicals and Iran also in play. The central item is whether the trade truce reached in 2025 gets extended. Preparatory talks have covered possible cooperation on AI safety and tariff reductions on some non-sensitive products; Chinese officials anticipate an announcement of roughly $30 billion in duty-free or low-tariff imports in exchange for purchase commitments made in May.

Why it matters: Tariff schedules on non-sensitive goods set input costs for a large share of American small businesses — anyone importing equipment, parts or finished stock. A truce extension holds current costs in place; a lapse changes them. The concrete dates to watch are Wednesday and Thursday.

Markets September 18–21

Stocks opened the week higher as oil fell and yields stayed high

S&P 500 futures advanced 0.6% and Nasdaq 100 contracts 0.8% into Monday's open, with technology leading and Treasuries climbing across the curve. Brent crude fell for a fourth consecutive session, the longest such stretch in three months, which is doing most of the work on the inflation side of the picture.

That follows a rough prior week. The Dow fell Friday and posted its worst week since March, with the S&P 500 also down on the week, as investors worked through rising yields on a triple-witching session. The September 18 par curve closed at 4.41% on the 1-year, 4.75% on the 2-year, 4.86% on the 5-year, 5.01% on the 10-year and 5.33% on the 30-year — roughly 25 basis points between the 2-year and the 10-year, four days after the FOMC's 12-0 vote to raise the target range to 3.75–4.00%.

Worth flagging as a genuine split rather than smoothing it over: the Committee wrote on September 16 that inflation remains elevated and raised rates, while in the same window market-implied inflation compensation fell, with the 5-year breakeven moving from 2.40% to 2.31%.

Why it matters: A 10-year above 5% sets the floor under mortgage, commercial real estate and small-business borrowing costs regardless of what equities do on any given day. The flat 2s10s spread is the part to watch, because that is what compresses lender margins and tightens credit availability for exactly the borrowers who need it.

◆ Policy with a small-business effect

Deadline today September 21

The SBA's rewrite of "small business" closes for comment

The SBA published two interrelated proposed rules on August 20 that would restructure federal small-business size standards more heavily than any change in decades. The methodology rule consolidates roughly 1,000 industry-specific standards into 338 and raises thresholds across the board; for professional services, information technology and engineering, thresholds would rise as much as tenfold or more, and the framework shifts toward headcount.

By the SBA's own estimate the proposal would newly classify about 114,541 businesses as small, including 37,002 firms that held fiscal 2025 federal contracts worth roughly $71 billion. Comments on both rules are due today, September 21, at regulations.gov under Docket No. SBA-2026-0199 for the standards and Docket No. SBA-2026-0265 for the methodology. As of September 1 the agency had logged more than 65,000 comments on the size-standards rule.

Why it matters: Size standards decide who can bid on set-aside federal contracts and who qualifies for several SBA loan programs. Raising them lets much larger firms compete inside the small-business set-aside pool, which changes the odds for the genuinely small firms already in it. Today is the last day the public record stays open.

Health policy September 18

Most-favored-nation drug pricing extended to every state Medicaid program

The administration announced on September 18 that all 50 states, the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico have joined the GENEROUS Medicaid payment model. Under it, participating manufacturers pay state Medicaid programs rebates on expensive brand-name drugs so the final price does not exceed a most-favored-nation benchmark tied to what comparable developed nations pay. The White House puts the projected savings at nearly $65 billion over the next decade and says 26 large drugmakers, covering about 90% of the branded US market, have agreed to align US prices with the lowest paid abroad. The model builds on a May 2025 executive order.

Why it matters: Medicaid drug spending is a direct state budget line, and lower outlays free room in state budgets that also fund the programs small businesses touch. For anyone tracking health costs personally, the mechanism here is a rebate to the payer rather than a price cut at the counter, so retail and commercial-insurance prices are not what changed.
