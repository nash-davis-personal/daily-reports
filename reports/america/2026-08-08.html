The economy shed 23,000 jobs in July and the market closed at a record, because a weak print is the bullish outcome now

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Friday morning that July nonfarm payrolls fell by 23,000, against forecasts clustered around 80,000 gains, and the S&P 500 and Nasdaq both closed at records on it — the tell that this cycle's fear is a rate hike rather than a cut. Underneath the headline the picture is split: the same morning the NFIB reported small-business hiring plans at their highest level since October 2022, and the unemployment rate fell to 4.1% on fewer people looking for work rather than more people finding it.

◆ The labor market and the Fed

Data August 7

July payrolls came in negative, and that is why stocks rallied

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported the economy lost 23,000 nonfarm jobs in July. Consensus going in was for roughly 80,000 added, with some surveys as high as 83,000, so the miss is on the order of 100,000 jobs. The unemployment rate came in at 4.1%, a tick below the 4.2% economists expected, but the mechanism matters: participation fell, so the rate improved because people left the labor force rather than because more of them found work.

Markets read it as removing the near-term risk of a rate increase. The S&P 500 rose 0.62% to a record 7,757.64, the Nasdaq gained 1.30% to a record 26,690.62, and the Dow added 151.83 points to 54,036.93. The 10-year Treasury yield fell to 4.64%. Technology led, which is the standard response when the rate path softens. Gold also rose sharply, which is the standard response when investors want a hedge at the same time.

Why it matters: A market that rallies on job losses is telling you what it is scared of, and right now it is inflation and higher rates rather than a slowdown. For anyone managing their own money, the practical read is that the equity move was driven by the discount rate rather than by earnings expectations, and a hot CPI print on Tuesday can reverse the same logic in one session.

Data August 7

Small-business hiring turned back up in July, which cuts against the national headline

NFIB released its July jobs report the same morning. The Small Business Employment Index rose to 102.1 from 100.2 in June, ending four consecutive months of decline and coming in above both the 2025 average of 101.2 and the historical average of 100.0. A seasonally adjusted 36% of owners reported job openings they could not fill, up four points and the highest since June 2025, with 31% looking for skilled workers and 14% for unskilled. A net 20% plan to create new jobs over the next three months, up nine points from June, the highest reading since October 2022 and nine points above the historical average.

Compensation moved with it. A net 31% reported raising pay, up three points. NFIB chief economist Bill Dunkelberg said labor quality or availability rose as the top concern for Main Street in July, "with employment growth falling behind the pace of economic growth."

Why it matters: The two datasets are measuring different things and both can be true — large employers pulling back while small firms with unfilled roles try to staff up. Unfilled openings at the highest level in over a year, alongside rising pay, is the condition under which owners start buying tools that let them get more out of the people they already have.

Calendar August 12–13

July CPI on Tuesday is the last real input before the September FOMC

July CPI publishes Tuesday, August 12, with July PPI the following day. Those are the inflation reads that carry into the FOMC meeting on September 15–16. The setup is unusual: the Fed held at 3.50%–3.75% on July 29 in a 9–3 vote, its most divided decision since 2016, and all three dissents wanted to raise rather than cut.

The complicating variable is energy. Oil ran up roughly 21% in July on Iran-related disruption to shipping, and that feeds into consumer prices with a lag, so a July print can pick up June and July's crude move even as prices come back down. Long-term inflation swaps still imply about 2.4% average inflation and 30-year Treasury yields are near 20-year highs, both signs the market has not settled the question.

Why it matters: A weak jobs number takes a September hike off the table only if inflation cooperates. If Tuesday runs hot, you get the uncomfortable combination of falling employment and a central bank still leaning toward tightening, and the rally that happened Friday reverses on the same logic that produced it.

◆ Fires, fuel, and the physical economy

Update August 7

Crews now have containment lines around all three Spokane-area fires

Firefighters reported perimeter containment lines established around all three fires burning in and around Spokane, Washington, and shifted to reinforcing them. Containment as of Friday: Autumn Lane at 37%, Fairview at 31%, Old Trails at 23%. The complex has burned more than 10,000 acres and 921 structures have been damaged or destroyed, with over 700 lost outright.

Roughly 65,000 people have been evacuated, and more than a thousand homes remain under orders, with the highest-level evacuations across parts of north and northwest Spokane including the Indian Trail area and communities near Riverside State Park and Nine Mile Falls. 1,986 fire personnel from 176 jurisdictions are working the incident. Nationally, 45,502 wildfires have burned more than 5.6 million acres so far this year, and at least 38% of the United States and Puerto Rico was in drought as of July 28.

Why it matters: Containment percentages rising is the first genuinely good news out of Spokane in a week, but 65,000 evacuees and 921 damaged structures is a regional economic event that outlasts the fire. Small businesses that close during a wildfire frequently do not reopen, and the insurance and rebuilding cycle runs for years.

Energy August 7

Crude finished the week sharply lower even after Friday's bounce, and Hormuz terms are still unsettled

Oil rose about 1% on Friday to settle near $78.20 a barrel, with Brent up roughly 1.3%, but the week still finished down more than 7%. The move down came on progress toward fuller reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, which had been effectively closed since February 28 before a June 18 memorandum of understanding between the United States and Iran.

The terms are not final. Iran's parliament is reviewing a draft that would set stricter conditions on commercial transit: barring US and Israeli vessels, requiring compensation from countries it designates as hostile before granting passage, and penalties on violators equal to 20% of cargo value, with a full reopening tied to lifting the US maritime blockade. On the consumer side, the EIA projects US average retail gasoline around $3.60 a gallon for the second half of 2026, down from $4.48 in May.

Why it matters: Roughly a dollar a gallon off the May peak is real money back into household budgets and into the cost base of every business that runs a vehicle. It also cuts the other way for the inflation print: the July CPI will still be picking up July's crude spike, so lower pump prices now do not guarantee a soft number Tuesday.
