◆ The fire season

Wildfire August 9

National preparedness is at Level 5, with 94 uncontained large fires and foreign crews on the line

The Incident Management Situation Report issued at 0730 MDT this morning puts the country at National Preparedness Level 5, the top of the scale. Initial attack activity is described as light at 183 fires, but the standing load is heavy: 8 new large incidents, 4 large fires contained, 94 uncontained large fires, 27 complex incident management teams committed and 1 national incident management organization. Across all geographic areas there are 147 active incidents covering 3,629,723 cumulative acres, with 645 crews, 1,870 engines, 226 helicopters and 30,419 personnel. Total personnel fell by 217 from the previous report.

The Northwest Coordination Center carries the bulk at 53 incidents and 2,629,170 acres with 18,428 personnel, and it is at PL 5 on its own. The Great Basin is the other PL 5 area at 628,354 acres. Several named fires show extreme behavior: Wrights Spring on the Fremont-Winema National Forest two miles west of Sprague River, Oregon, with crowning and long-range spotting; Little Giant on the Okanogan-Wenatchee thirty miles northwest of Leavenworth, Washington; and Sinlahekin one mile west of Tonasket, Washington, with short crown runs and group torching. Numerous structures and critical infrastructure are threatened at each, with evacuations in effect.

Why it matters: Preparedness Level 5 means national resources are committed and competition for crews, aircraft and equipment is at its maximum, which is the operational reason foreign teams and military airtankers get called in. For anyone with travel, property or family in Oregon, Washington or the northern Great Basin, closures and evacuation orders are active and changing daily.
NIFC Incident Management Situation Report
Follow-up August 9

The three Spokane-area fires are calmer but evacuations have not lifted

Old Trails three miles west of Spokane, Autumn Lane seven miles northwest, and Fairview two miles northeast are all reported with moderate fire behavior described as backing, creeping and smoldering. All three remain under evacuations with area, road and trail closures, and numerous structures and critical infrastructure are still listed as threatened. Old Trails shows a reduction in acreage, which the report attributes to more accurate mapping rather than to progress on the fire.

The same Washington cluster includes Modrite, Bradeen Hill, Mitre Rock and Hudspeth in the northeast region, several with energy infrastructure threatened and evacuations in place. One incident management team is running four of those fires at once.

Why it matters: Containment lines going in around a fire is different from an evacuation being lifted, and this is the gap where people return home early. The acreage revision is also a useful reminder that a fire shrinking on a map can be a measurement correction.
NIFC Incident Management Situation Report

◆ Weather ahead

Tropical August 9

Forecasters give a Central Pacific system a 90 percent chance of developing within a week

The Central Pacific Tropical Weather Outlook issued at 2:00 AM HST tracks a tropical wave roughly 1,000 miles east-southeast of the Hawaiian Islands producing disorganized showers and thunderstorms. Formation odds are low at 30 percent through 48 hours and high at 90 percent through seven days, with a tropical depression expected to develop as the system moves west-northwestward. A second wave several hundred miles southwest of the southern tip of Baja California is at 30 percent and 60 percent over the same windows.

AccuWeather meteorologists have pointed to August 10 through 12 as the likely development window, with steering winds that could bring effects to Hawaii around August 14 and 15. That far out, track and intensity are not settled, and the current outlook does not forecast a landfall.

Why it matters: A 90 percent seven-day formation chance is high confidence that something forms and low confidence about where it goes. NOAA's outlook for this season called for an above-normal central Pacific with 5 to 13 tropical cyclones, so this is the season behaving as forecast rather than a surprise.

◆ The economy this week

Data August 11

July CPI arrives Tuesday, two trading days after a negative payroll print

Friday's Employment Situation release showed nonfarm payrolls down 23,000 in July with the unemployment rate at 4.1 percent, the first monthly decline since February and well short of expectations near a gain of 86,000. Markets read the weak labor print as lowering the odds of further tightening. July CPI on Tuesday is the last major scheduled input before the September FOMC meeting.

The setup is a simple two-variable problem. A soft CPI alongside a negative payroll number points one direction for policy; a firm CPI against the same labor data points at the harder case, where growth is slowing while prices are not.

Why it matters: For anyone managing their own money, Tuesday morning is the scheduled volatility, and positioning ahead of a print is a bet on the number rather than on the economy. For small businesses, the labor reading matters more than the market reaction: a softer hiring market changes what you have to pay to fill a role.
