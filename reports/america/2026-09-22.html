◆ The economy

Energy September 21

Oil hit a 12-day low; pump prices are still going up

Crude eased on Monday to its lowest level in 12 days. Investors priced in the possibility of diplomatic progress on the Iran war around this week's United Nations meetings, after the US president said he was open to talks with Iran there, and factored in a partial recovery in Saudi export shipments. US benchmark light sweet crude fell 5.4% in late-afternoon trading to $95.94 a barrel, about 9.9% below its September 15 peak of $106.52. Brent closed at $100.06, down 3.67% on the day.

Refined products have not followed. Diesel averaged $6.511 a gallon, up 28 cents — 4.5% — in a week and 16.3% since the start of September. Gasoline is also still rising. Crude and pump prices decouple for weeks at a time because refining margins, inventories and regional supply move on their own clock, so a barrel that fell yesterday does not reach a fuel tank today.

Why it matters: Diesel is a direct input cost for the trades, delivery, landscaping and contracting businesses that make up much of the Charlotte-area small-business base. A 16% move inside one month shows up as a price increase, a fuel surcharge, or a thinner margin within weeks. Falling crude is not relief until it reaches the rack.

Why it matters: A record index close driven by a handful of semiconductor names is narrow strength, not broad strength. For anyone managing their own money, the practical read is that index-level gains and portfolio-level gains can diverge sharply when leadership concentrates — worth knowing what share of your own exposure sits in the same few names that moved the tape.

◆ Infrastructure and public safety

Security Confirmed September 18

Two Colorado water utilities were reached, and the national count keeps rising

Colorado officials confirmed that foreign actors accessed two small water utility systems in late August. The intruders changed equipment settings, disabled remote access and alarms, and altered pumping cycles. The affected systems serve roughly 400 people, and the state said drinking water quality and treatment were not affected.

The wider pattern: the EPA counts more than 100 drinking-water and wastewater systems across 12 states targeted this year, including utilities in Minnesota, Michigan, Georgia, South Dakota and New Jersey. The FBI and CISA warned that the activity has escalated in recent weeks across at least seven states. The common entry point is internet-facing programmable logic controllers, where attackers change IP addresses and passwords and take away an operator's ability to monitor or control the plant. Federal guidance is to disconnect these systems from the internet where possible and to make sure staff can revert to manual control.

Why it matters: The target set is small utilities with thin IT staffing, which describes most municipal systems in North Carolina as well as Colorado. The documented impact so far is loss of monitoring and control rather than contaminated water, and federal agencies are treating it as an active campaign.

◆ Policy with a concrete effect

Rulemaking Comments closed September 21

The SBA wants to redraw who counts as a small business

Two interrelated proposed rules published in the Federal Register on August 20 would consolidate nearly 1,000 industry-specific size standards into 338 and raise the thresholds substantially — by as much as tenfold or more in professional services, information technology and engineering. Public comment closed September 21.

By the SBA's own estimate, roughly 114,541 businesses would newly qualify as small, including about 37,002 firms that held fiscal 2025 federal contracts worth approximately $71 billion. Nothing is final; the agency now reviews comments before issuing any final rule.

Why it matters: The set-aside pool for federal contracts is fixed while the number of firms eligible to compete for it would grow. For a genuinely small firm that currently wins small-business set-asides, the practical effect is more competitors of larger size in the same bracket. For a firm just over today's threshold, it is the opposite.

◆ Science and space

Space September 21

Super Heavy rolled to the pad; Albania signed the Artemis Accords

SpaceX moved its Super Heavy booster to the pad at Starbase in Texas on September 21, ahead of what would be the first orbital Starship mission. The launch is targeted for no earlier than September 28 and still needs final FAA approval. Separately, the Republic of Albania signed the Artemis Accords at NASA Headquarters in Washington on September 21, becoming the 73rd signatory.

Why it matters: A successful orbital Starship flight changes the cost assumptions behind every large payload program in the US, commercial and government. The date is not fixed — FAA approval is still outstanding.
