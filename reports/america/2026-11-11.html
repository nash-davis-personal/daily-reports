◆ The economy

Inflation Nov 10

October CPI rose 0.4%, core 0.3%, and for the first time the report carried no 12-month headline rate

The Bureau of Labor Statistics said the all-items index rose 0.4% in October on a seasonally adjusted basis after 0.6% in September; the Dow Jones consensus was 0.3%. Core, which strips out food and energy, rose 0.3%. Shelter rose 0.3%, food 0.5% (food at home 0.4%, away from home 0.5%), and energy 0.7% after a 5.2% jump in September. The release states that BLS did not collect survey data for October 2025 because of a lapse in appropriations, so 12-month changes cannot be published for the all-items index or most components. The few 12-month figures available show gasoline up 37.4%, used cars and trucks down 3.3% and new vehicles up 0.7%. The next CPI, for November, is scheduled for Dec 10. The NFIB's October survey, released the same morning, showed small-business optimism nearly unchanged with uncertainty lower, and owners reporting that they are raising prices faster than normal to offset costs.

Why it matters: Prices are rising at an annualized pace well above the Fed's target, and the missing annual rate means every "inflation is X%" headline this month is an estimate. For a household, the 37% gasoline figure is the number that shows up at the pump; for a small business, the NFIB read says competitors are already passing costs through.

Rates Nov 10

Futures put a December hike at 88% and a half-point move at 11%; the 10-year yield rose toward 5% and the 2-year jumped 17 basis points

CME FedWatch pricing after the CPI release showed an 88% probability of a quarter-point increase at December's meeting, up from 77% a week earlier, and an 11% probability of a half point, up from zero. BMO's Ian Lyngen said Fed Governor Christopher Waller had suggested a 50 basis point hike "will be discussed in the context of ensuring Fed credibility." The 10-year Treasury yield rose nearly 10 basis points to about 4.95% and briefly crossed 5% intraday per several reports; CNN called it the largest surge in yields in 19 months. The 2-year rose 17 basis points on the day and the 30-year was reported at 5.358% at one point. The October jobs report on Nov 6 had already lifted hike odds with payrolls up 165,000 and wages up 3.1% year over year. Bitcoin fell to a near three-month low and gold retreated on the day.

Why it matters: Borrowing costs are moving up again. A 30-year mortgage tracks the 10-year yield, so anyone shopping a house, a car loan or a business line of credit is looking at higher rates into year end; savers in money-market funds get the other side of that.

Stocks rallied 2.4% Monday after last week's correction, then slipped Tuesday as tech led a retreat; Nvidia fell on a board investigation and reports earnings Nov 17

Monday, Nov 9: the Dow rose 2.2% to 52,389.75 and the S&P 500 gained 2.4% to 7,549.94, clawing back part of the Nov 5 drop that had put the Nasdaq into a correction. Nvidia closed down 4.27% Monday after its board announced an independent investigation into CEO Jensen Huang on Sunday following reports he was targeted by a foreign intelligence effort; the board said it has no reason to believe trade secrets or national security were compromised, and the stock fell further Tuesday while AMD and Broadcom rose about 3%. ◆ Energy and the world's chokepoint

Oil Nov 10–11

Hormuz attacks hit a monthly record of 37 vessels in October; oil fell to a two-month low Monday and rebounded Tuesday to about $92.50 Brent as Iran said no talks were under way

Oil fell more than $3 a barrel on Monday after US officials downplayed the need for further strikes, then recovered after an Iranian security official said no talks were taking place between the two sides. On Tuesday the UK Maritime Trade Operations agency said a tanker was struck northeast of Lima, Oman, and Iran's Revolutionary Guards Navy said two tankers caught fire after hitting naval mines on what it called an unauthorized route through the strait. Data from the Washington Institute for Near East Policy counted 37 vessels attacked in the Strait of Hormuz and surrounding waters in October, the highest monthly total since the war began, with little sign of slowing in November. By Wednesday morning Brent was near $92.53 and WTI near $86.71. LSEG analysts said that even with a deal, flows through the strait would depend on mine clearance, insurance costs and shipowners' willingness to sail; one tracker counted three transits on Nov 8 against about 85 a day in normal times. At home, the EIA now expects record US electricity consumption in both 2026 and 2027, rising from 4,216 billion kWh in 2025 to 4,370 billion kWh in 2027, partly on AI data centers.

Why it matters: The strait carries about a fifth of the world's seaborne oil, and the October CPI's 37% annual gasoline figure is what a closed chokepoint looks like at a US pump. Record power demand means utility rate cases, including Duke Energy's pending 11.9% request in North Carolina for January 2027, are the next bill to watch.

◆ Charlotte and North Carolina

Local Nov 9

Charlotte City Council approved the 10.5-year CLT airline lease 8–2, unlocking a $2.1 billion expansion with a Concourse C build-out and new international arrivals

After a 6–5 vote to delay, a mayoral veto (the first in at least 13 years per WFAE) and a declined override, the council voted Monday night to approve a lease with American Airlines, Delta, Frontier, Southwest and United that takes effect Jan 1, 2027, runs 10.5 years with a five-year option, and keeps the existing revenue split of 55% to the city and 45% to the airlines. The city estimates about $3 billion in rent over the life of the deal. The lease backs the "CLT Beyond" capital program of roughly $2.1 billion: a major Concourse C expansion, new gates, a passenger tunnel, international-arrivals upgrades and airfield work, funded without Charlotte property taxes. Council members JD Mazuera Arias and Reneé Perkins Johnson voted no over wages and conditions for subcontracted airport workers, and service workers walked out during the meeting. Senator Thom Tillis had said he would push for an independent authority to run the airport if the council rejected the lease. American's CEO Robert Isom said the airline has grown alongside Charlotte for more than 40 years.

Why it matters: CLT is the region's largest economic engine and American's second-largest hub; a decade of certainty plus $2.1 billion in construction means years of contractor, hospitality and logistics work on the west side of the county, and it settles a fight that had drawn in Raleigh and Washington.

◆ Weather and space

Weather Nov 12–14

A Northeast storm Thursday night through Saturday, then the coldest air since spring for nearly 200 million people east of the Mississippi; a high-risk heavy-rain and mountain-snow setup in the Pacific Northwest and northern California through Thursday

A storm forming over the central US is expected to strengthen as it reaches the Atlantic, bringing rain, gusty winds, some coastal flooding and higher-elevation snow to the Northeast from Thursday night into Saturday; forecasters say impacts should be less severe and widespread than September's nor'easter, and models still disagree on the exact track, which decides how much snow falls in New England's hills. Behind it, nearly 200 million people east of the Mississippi are expected to see their coldest temperatures since spring, the first real cold of the season for the Carolinas. On the West Coast, the Weather Prediction Center flagged a high risk of heavy precipitation for parts of the Pacific Northwest and northern California from Tuesday through Thursday, Nov 10–12, with heavy high-elevation snow in the Washington Cascades and possible lower-elevation flooding. South Texas was under a flood threat from heavy rain. These are forecasts; no major damage had been reported at filing.

Why it matters: The first hard cold of the year is when furnaces fail and pipes burst, which means HVAC and plumbing phones ring and travel through the Northeast corridor slows Thursday night into Saturday.

Space Nov 7–13

Starship Flight 15 targets Friday with the first attempt to catch both the booster and the Ship; NASA named eight companies for a $10 billion lunar-delivery contract and booked Starship for 2028 Moon communications

SpaceX is targeting Friday, Nov 13 at 5:45 p.m. ET from Starbase, Texas, with a 75-minute window and pending regulatory approval, to launch Starship Flight 15 and attempt to catch the Super Heavy booster and then the Ship with the tower's arms, which has never been done. A slip or a waved-off catch is possible. On Nov 7 NASA announced eight companies eligible to compete under CLPS 2, a 10-year commercial lunar delivery contract with a combined ceiling of $10 billion, and separately tasked SpaceX under an existing contract to deploy communications satellites into cislunar space with Starship in 2028. The same week SpaceX flew a classified Space Force mission from Vandenberg (USSF-432 on Nov 6 was its 125th launch of 2026) with two more scheduled, ULA's Vulcan was set to return to flight with 40 Amazon Leo satellites and Rocket Lab was set to fly its 100th Electron.

Why it matters: A fully recovered Starship is the step that makes a reusable heavy-lift rocket an everyday vehicle, and the CLPS 2 roster is the list of companies that will haul cargo to a Moon base this decade.
