◆ Science & space

Milestone Jul 24–25, 2026

Starship Flight 13 put working satellites in orbit and came back mostly intact

The 407-foot Super Heavy and Starship stack lifted off from SpaceX's Starbase site in Texas at 5:51 p.m. EDT on 33 methane-fueled Raptor engines and roughly 16 million pounds of thrust, after an earlier attempt was scrubbed on tropical storm conditions and engine ignition work. Ship 40 deployed the first batch of Starlink V3 satellites, the first time Starship has delivered functioning payloads to orbit rather than mass simulators.

Stage separation and the flip were clean. The upper stage completed its objectives and made the program's softest vertical splashdown to date, staying largely intact and floating after toppling into the water, which gave engineers extended views of heatshield condition. The Super Heavy booster was the weak point: its landing burn lit 10 Raptors, stepped down to eight and then five, and the vehicle came in too fast for a successful splashdown.

Why it matters: Starship's value to anyone outside SpaceX depends on it carrying cargo, and this is the flight where it did. Starlink V3 satellites are the payload that scales the constellation's capacity, which bears on rural broadband availability across the Carolinas and everywhere else terrestrial service is thin. The booster's landing shortfall keeps full reusability unproven.

◆ Economy & energy

Prices Jul 24, 2026

Gasoline is back above $4 a gallon as Hormuz shipping disruption feeds through

National average gasoline prices moved back above $4 a gallon, the first time in more than a month, after disruption to tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz tightened crude supply. Roughly a fifth of the world's oil moves through that channel in normal conditions. Crude has been volatile rather than one-directional: prices pushed above $100 a barrel earlier in the week, then WTI fell 2.25% to $90.12 on Friday and Brent dropped about 4% to trade below $96 following a Reuters report that Pakistan is weighing new peace talks between the United States and Iran.

The United States and United Kingdom are planning a high-level meeting in London this week to discuss an international coalition to protect commercial shipping through the strait. Friday night was the first in 13 consecutive nights without US strikes on Iran.

Why it matters: Fuel is the fastest-moving input cost most small businesses have. Anything with a vehicle — trades, delivery, mobile services, a detailing route — is absorbing this now, and it shows up in customer pricing within weeks. Higher energy costs also feed the inflation prints the Fed is reading, which is the link between a shipping lane and a mortgage rate.

Week ahead Jul 29–30, 2026

Four rate signals compress into two days

The FOMC decision lands Wednesday, July 29 at 2:00 p.m. ET. The Bureau of Economic Analysis then releases the Q2 GDP advance estimate and the June PCE price index together on Thursday, July 30, hours after the Fed has already spoken. Big Tech earnings fill the gap between them. The backdrop: June headline CPI at 3.5% year over year, unemployment at 4.2%, and a June Summary of Economic Projections showing the Fed penciling in 2.2% real GDP growth and 3.6% PCE inflation for 2026.

Markets went into it mixed. Friday closed with the S&P 500 at 7,411.98, up 0.05%, the Dow up 235.60 points to 51,947.25, and the Nasdaq down 0.64% to 24,975.82 on continued weakness in chip names.

Why it matters: If the Fed signals one thing Wednesday and Thursday's inflation and growth data say another, positioning has to move fast, and that volatility reaches ordinary balances through retirement accounts and any variable-rate borrowing. For a small business, the practical read is on credit: the path of short rates over the next two quarters is what sets the cost of a line of credit or an equipment loan.

◆ Weather & the Carolinas

Update Jul 23–26, 2026

Bertha's moisture clears the Carolinas after flash flooding and scattered outages

Tropical Storm Bertha dissipated over Texas, but its moisture plume kept a flood threat over the Southeast into the weekend, with a Level 2 of 4 flooding-rainfall risk running from the coastal Carolinas inland to Fayetteville, North Carolina and Sumter, South Carolina, and widespread totals of 2 to 4 inches with isolated spots above 5. Flooding briefly closed a section of Interstate 40 northwest of Durham on Thursday morning, and remnant rain pushed the Roanoke River up. Duke Energy's outage map showed close to 2,500 customers out in East Durham, plus an outage near Weston Road in Garner from fallen trees.

The damage was lighter than the rainfall totals suggested. An existing serious drought across much of the region absorbed a lot of it, and the rain arrived prolonged rather than intense in the worst-hit areas.

Why it matters: A multi-day rain event dropping into drought-hardened ground is the setup that produces flash flooding on roads before it produces river flooding, which is what closed I-40. For anyone running a route-based business in the Charlotte metro, the operational lesson is that the risk window is the first few hours of a storm on dry ground, not the day after.
