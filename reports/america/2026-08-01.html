◆ Infrastructure and public safety

Breaking July 31

Federal agencies warned water utilities nationwide after break-ins in at least seven states

The FBI and the Environmental Protection Agency issued a warning to water utilities across the country after systems in at least seven states reported intrusions to the FBI beginning July 27. The agencies did not name the states. The FBI's public service announcement identified the targeted equipment specifically: Allen-Bradley MicroLogix 1100 and 1400 series programmable logic controllers made by Rockwell Automation, internet-facing industrial computers that manage pumps, valves and pressure monitoring at water facilities. The FBI reported that some utilities lost water pressure and others experienced flooding.

A Minnesota law enforcement memo states that the intent went beyond taking systems offline and extended to contaminating the drinking water supply. The warning follows an earlier incident in which more than 30 Minnesota municipal water facilities were targeted; a law enforcement official said that attack carried hallmarks of Iranian involvement.

Why it matters: This is a direct operational risk to a basic utility rather than a data-theft event. The named hardware is a common, decades-old controller line found at small municipal utilities with limited IT staff and budget, which is why the federal advisory went to every water system rather than a short list. For residents, the practical takeaway is to follow local utility notices about pressure or boil-water advisories; for anyone running facilities on similar industrial controllers, the advisory names the exact models to get off the public internet.

◆ Markets and the cost of money

Markets July 31

Stocks capped a volatile July higher as Amazon jumped 15% and Apple fell 7%

All three major indexes finished the month's last session up. The S&P 500 added 0.7% to close at 7,489.72, the Nasdaq Composite rose about 1% to 25,373.85, and the Dow gained 276.97 points, or 0.53%, to 52,485.03 — a 0.7% gain for July and a fourth straight winning month. All three posted weekly gains.

The split inside the tape was wide. Amazon rose 15% on earnings that beat expectations, with its chip business expanding. Apple fell 7% after services and China revenue came in short. Microsoft's 15% rally the day before set the week's tone. Between them, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta and Alphabet now guide to $720–745 billion of combined 2026 capital spending. University of Michigan data showed a broad pickup in consumer sentiment despite earlier concern about energy prices.

Why it matters: The index gain conceals a market that is separating winners from losers on a single question — who is capturing returns on AI spending rather than just funding it. A 22-point spread between two of the largest companies in the world in one session is a concentration risk for anyone holding a cap-weighted index fund and assuming it is diversified. Roughly three-quarters of a trillion dollars of committed 2026 capex also means this concentration is not resolving soon.

Rates July 30–31

The 10-year hit a one-year high and mortgage rates rose for a fourth straight week

The 10-year Treasury yield jumped to 4.73%, its highest level in more than a year, as investors reassessed the Fed's decision to hold rates without offering clear forward guidance. Freddie Mac's survey put the 30-year fixed mortgage at 6.66% for the week ending July 30, up from 6.58% and the fourth consecutive weekly increase. The 15-year averaged 6.04%, up from 5.96%. Both remain slightly below where they sat a year ago, when the 30-year averaged 6.72%.

Why it matters: The 10-year is the reference rate for a large share of consumer and business borrowing, so a one-year high pushes up mortgages, auto loans, small-business credit lines and the discount rate applied to future earnings. Four straight weekly increases in mortgage rates during peak summer buying season slows housing activity with a lag. For anyone carrying a variable-rate balance or planning to finance equipment this year, the cost of waiting has gone up.

Households July 30

Americans are spending faster than they earn, and the saving rate fell to a four-year low

June personal spending rose 0.3% while personal income rose 0.2%, with health care, motor vehicles, financial services and insurance driving the gain. Adjusted for inflation, real spending was up 0.4%, mostly services. The personal saving rate — the share of after-tax income households keep — slipped to 2.7%, its lowest in nearly four years. The same release showed the Fed's preferred inflation gauge down 0.1% for the month, putting the annual rate at 3.7% from 4.1% in May, with core PCE up 0.1% on the month and 3.3% year over year.

Why it matters: Consumer spending is roughly two-thirds of the economy, so the composition of that spending is a leading indicator. Spending funded by a shrinking savings buffer rather than rising income is harder to sustain, and it usually shows up first in discretionary categories. Small businesses selling anything optional should plan for a softer second half than the headline spending number suggests.

◆ Rules taking effect today

Policy August 1

California's one-request data deletion system became binding on data brokers

The Delete Act, S.B. 362, directed the California Privacy Protection Agency to build DROP — the Delete Request and Opt-Out Platform — a single place where a consumer submits one request to delete their non-exempt personal information across every registered data broker. Consumers have been able to file requests since January 1. As of August 1, brokers must act on them: check DROP at least once every 45 days, process each request within 45 days, and report status back through the platform. Failure to delete carries a penalty of $200 per deletion request for each day of non-compliance.

Why it matters: This is the first centralized, one-request deletion mechanism of its kind in the United States, and the compliance question reaches further than expected — the definition of "data broker" is broad enough that companies that never thought of themselves as one may be registered or required to register. California privacy rules have historically become the national floor because companies find it cheaper to apply one standard everywhere. Any California resident can file a single request today and have it propagate.
